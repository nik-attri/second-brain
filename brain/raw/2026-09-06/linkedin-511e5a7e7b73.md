Tech Evolution: Leadership at home and at work. 

An interesting conversation with my son this morning made me pause and think about evolution; not just of technology, but of the way we think.

I said:
“I worry about the way modern cars assist drivers. I wonder whether new drivers will ever develop the same depth of driving skills that previous generations had when there was less technology to rely on.”

His response caught me off guard:

“Isn’t that like saying people shouldn’t use computers at work because they make things easier, and new employees won’t know how to work without them?”

Touché.

What I saw as the potential loss of a skill, he saw as the natural evolution of how we accomplish things.

And perhaps that is one of the hardest things about watching our children grow: realising that the world they are preparing for is not necessarily the world we were prepared for.

Every generation inherits knowledge, tools and ways of doing things; and then improves, replaces or reimagines them.

Perhaps evolution is not about whether the next generation can do things exactly as we did.

Perhaps it is about whether they can adapt, think critically and use what is available to move forward.

And maybe the real lesson for me this morning was not about cars or technology at all.

It was about motherhood.

There comes a point when the child you taught to navigate the world starts challenging the way you see it.

And you realise, with a mixture of pride and humility, that you are no longer only teaching them. They are teaching you too.

I’d call our conversation this morning “The Assessment of Evolution.”

And yes… he may have won this round. 😂

————————————————————————

In the workplace, this creates an interesting tension between experience and evolution.

* Older ways of working: “I know how to do this manually, so I understand the process.”
* New ways of working: “I don’t need to do every step manually if technology can do it faster and better.”
* The real question: Do we understand what the technology is doing well enough to know when to trust it, when to challenge it, and when to take over?

LESSONS

1. Our responsibility isn’t to prepare the next generation to work the way we did. It’s to give them enough understanding of the past to make better decisions about the future.

2. Don’t teach people merely how to use the technology. Teach them how to think when using it.
