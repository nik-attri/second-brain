Private practice owners: stop automatically blaming the pissed-off patient who left you a 1-star Google review.

Seriously.

I hear it all the time:

“They were difficult.”

“They had unrealistic expectations.”

“They were never going to be happy.”

“They don’t understand how healthcare works.”

Maybe.

Or maybe your clinic f’d up.

That possibility should make you uncomfortable.

Because your Google reviews might be one of the most powerful quality-assurance tools you have—and too many owners treat them like nothing more than a marketing scorecard.

Forget the star rating for a second.

READ. THE. REVIEWS.

Your patients are literally telling you what it’s like to experience your business when you’re not standing in the room.

Your spreadsheets aren’t going to tell you that your front desk made someone feel like an inconvenience.

Your P&L isn’t going to tell you that a therapist made someone feel rushed.

Your productivity report isn’t going to tell you that nobody answered the damn phone.

Your cancellation report isn’t going to tell you that the clinic environment felt chaotic.

But your patients will.

And here’s where it gets really interesting:

Don’t just track the bad stuff.

Track the good stuff too.

If patients repeatedly mention the same therapist by name—why?

If one clinic constantly gets praised for its energy and environment—why?

If patients repeatedly mention how welcomed they felt—why?

If one employee keeps showing up in glowing reviews—what the hell are they doing differently?

That’s data.

Find it.

Study it.

Then replicate the hell out of it.

This is why we track Google reviews by clinic.

Not because we’re obsessed with collecting 5 stars.

Because reviews are operational intelligence.

Revenue tells me whether the business is making money.

Productivity tells me whether we’re efficient.

Cancellation rates tell me whether patients are showing up.

But Google reviews tell me how patients actually experience our company.

And I want the good, the bad, and the ugly.

Because I’d rather have a patient publicly tell me where we’re screwing up than have 50 patients quietly experience the same problem and never say a damn thing.

So the next time you get a 1-star review, resist the urge to immediately defend your practice.

Read it.

Investigate it.

Look for patterns.

Fix what needs fixing.

And if the review is bullsh%#?

Fine. Move on.

But if three, four, five patients start saying the same thing…

That’s not a difficult patient anymore.

That’s a leadership problem.
