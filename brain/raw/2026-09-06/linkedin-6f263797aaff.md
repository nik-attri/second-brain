After more than 25 years working across engineering, industry, renewable energy, EPC and quality management, I have come to a rather uncomfortable conclusion: many companies don’t really have a people problem. They have a bureaucracy problem disguised as organisation. We create meetings to decide when to have another meeting, emails to ask whether someone has read the previous email, spreadsheets that slowly become more important than the reality they are supposed to represent, and procedures designed by people who have never actually had to deal with the problems those procedures are meant to solve. Then, when something goes wrong, the first question is usually “Who is responsible?” rather than “Why did our system allow this to happen?”

To me, good project management is not about producing more reports, controlling more people or adding another layer of approval. It is about making the right decision, at the right time, with the right information — ideally before a small problem becomes an expensive one. And this is where I think we often confuse complexity with professionalism. They are not the same thing. Sometimes complexity is simply inefficiency that has been given a job title, a procedure and a meeting room.

Now we have AI, which can make almost everything faster. That is extraordinary. But there is an obvious risk: if you put AI into a badly designed organisation, you may simply end up with a badly designed organisation that makes mistakes much faster. Technology can accelerate a process. It cannot decide whether the process itself makes any sense. That still requires judgement, experience and, occasionally, the courage to say: “Why the hell are we still doing this?”

Perhaps real efficiency is not about doing more things in less time. Perhaps it is about having the intelligence to stop doing the things that never needed to be done in the first place.
