We didn't have a ticket problem. We had a hiring problem that could not be solved with hiring.

Simon Profi-Technik sells garden and forestry equipment online. Spring and summer bury the support team. Winter is quiet. Two extra people for the season would sit idle from October. Seasonal hires don't work either, because advising someone on a chainsaw takes real product knowledge and months of onboarding.

So every year the same small team absorbed the flood, and the complex cases waited. We accepted that for years. This year we didn't.

We built AI support straight into Zendesk: an n8n workflow, the current Gemini model, and a direct line to our ERP. It answers the questions that make up most of the pile. Where is my order, with the tracking link. Send me my invoice, as a PDF. Which accessory fits this machine, with a link to the right product. That last one works because we fed it a knowledge base for the brands we carry and trained it on anonymized past tickets.

Two decisions I would not skip if I did it again:

1. Customers can see the reply was written with AI assistance. No hiding it.
2. Every answer carries a confidence score. Below the threshold, or when a customer replies with something it can't handle, the ticket goes to a person. Same as it always did.

Results so far: it handles two to three times the volume of one human agent, around the clock, in season and out. The team now spends its time on complaints and technical advice, which is the work we hired them for in the first place.

Payback was weeks. Not because the technology is cheap. Because the alternative was two salaries we could never justify.

COO Insider asked for real automation stories with fast ROI and published mine this week alongside about two dozen others. Reading the rest, one pattern kept showing up: the fastest returns didn't come from replacing judgment. They came from removing the waiting. Worth thinking about before you automate anything. 

Where does the waiting hide in your process?
