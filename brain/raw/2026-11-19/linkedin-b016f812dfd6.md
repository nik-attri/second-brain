Somewhere in New South Wales this week there is wheat testing 14% protein and 70% screenings in the same parcel. Rain did that. Protein a miller pays for, screenings that make it feed.

The grower isn't selling yet. He'll clean it, blend it and try to deliver a grade higher. Sensible, because the grade is the margin: the gap between top milling wheat and standard white has opened to around A$60 a tonne in the north.
Now look at how a mid-sized desk holds that cargo.

The contract has a spec. Grade, protein, screenings, moisture. The trading system has a field for it, and that's where its knowledge of quality ends.

The actual quality arrives lot by lot: a receival docket, a lab report somebody forwarded, the grower's photo of a test strip. The blend that turns three off-spec lots into one contractual cargo exists in one person's spreadsheet. When the buyer's analysis comes back a month after discharge with a number the trader doesn't like, the dispute goes to whoever finds the right PDF first.
Most quality claims are an argument about a document nobody filed against the lot.

We think the test result belongs on the lot record from the day it's sampled, next to the receipt, with the contract spec and the discharge certificate alongside it. An oilseeds group that grows, crushes, stores and trades told us recently that tying quality certificates to each trade is near the top of their list. We weren't surprised.
