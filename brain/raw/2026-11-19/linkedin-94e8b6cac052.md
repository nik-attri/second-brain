The Modern Tradition of Architecture Through Its Houses 
  
 
  
Some people think houses are built with brick, concrete and steel. 
  
Lies. 
  
 
  
They are built with fear, with ambition, with other people’s ideas stolen on purpose, and with the shadow of whatever is happening outside, in the street, while the architect, who is no fool, pretends not to notice.
  
 
  
Look at the dates. There they are, unvarnished.
  
Wright lays      the Robie House across the American prairie, while the world meets      Bakelite and the Model T starts rolling.
  
Mies signs      the Tugendhat House, all light, glass and serenity. And in New York the      stock market goes to hell.
  
Wright hangs      a house over a waterfall. The following year, Spain begins to kill itself.
  
Mies      conceives a glass box, the Farnsworth House, to live in the open air.      Hiroshima has just taught us how fragile everything is.
  
 
  
 
  
Coincidence, you’ll say. I don’t buy it. No house is born alone. It comes into the world carrying its century on its back: its paintings, its chairs, its inventions and its disasters. And almost nobody tells us all of it together.
  
So I did.
  
 
  
95 houses. From Palladio’s Villa Capra, in 1570, to 2026. 966 images. Each dwelling with its context: the art being painted, the chair being sat in, the discovery that changed life and the event that twisted it. A timeline to walk through slowly, the way you walk through a cemetery full of names that still speak to us.
  
 
  
A spreadsheet. Yes, an Excel file. No glitter, no smoke. Real work inside: a year-by-year timeline, a card for every house, the full text of each one, and a gallery where every image enlarges with a click. Morris and Webb’s Red House here, Casa Malaparte there, Aravena’s Quinta Monroy further on. There is the thread, and it lets itself be pulled.
  
 
  
 
  
What is historical analysis for? 
  
 
  
Something very simple and very uncomfortable: knowing where we come from. We architects have a bad habit of looking ahead with the arrogance of those who think nothing was done before they arrived. It was. And better than we suppose. Whoever ignores the past is not free, just a repeat offender. Knowing the memory of the craft doesn’t chain you to it; it lets you break it with judgment, the only honest way to innovate.
