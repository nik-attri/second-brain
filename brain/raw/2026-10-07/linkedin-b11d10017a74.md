I HAVE MADE THINGS UP FOR A LIVING.
Not the numbers. There are rules about that too.

I mean products.

For a surprisingly large portion of my career, my job has involved looking at something that doesn't exist and deciding if it should.

This is considerably less scientific than it sounds. Sometimes there's research. Sometimes there's data. Sometimes there's a trend you've been watching for months. And sometimes someone walks into a meeting and says:

“I've been thinking about Dave.”

Dave, you may remember, is a widget.

Except this is Young Dave. Embryonic Dave. Dave does not yet exist.

At this point Dave is approximately 4 lines in a PowerPoint, an alarming margin assumption, a sample that is the wrong colour, wrong size and made from something we definitely cannot afford.

And everyone has opinions about him. Strong opinions.

Sales thinks Dave needs another feature, which they will explain in highly technical language that absolutely nobody buying Dave will understand.

Marketing thinks Dave needs to be blue. Not sky blue. Not a blue with an actual Pantone reference. Positano beach blue.

Finance would like to know why Dave exists. Dave's older brother was in the range 5 years ago and did NOT fly. Finance remembers.

Operations has just discovered Dave requires a completely different carton. In pink. So he can be quickly identified in the container, efficiently removed and relocated to Warehouse 5, Bay 17.

The supplier says Dave is absolutely possible, provided we order 40,000 Dave's.

The retailer isn't entirely convinced about Dave. Which is problematic because everyone else is becoming quite attached to him. They like Old Dave. In black. People buy Old Dave.

And Product is sitting in the middle trying to work out whether anyone will actually buy Dave and all his little friends.

This is roughly the point where Dave begins to acquire a ‘business case’.

Dave gets researched. Costed. Re-costed. Simplified. Complicated again. Shown to consumers. Sent back to the supplier. Returned looking inexplicably different. Forecast. Re-forecast. Argued about.

Robust discussions are had. Then had again.

At some point someone will ask how many Dave's were going to sell.

Nobody knows.

This does not prevent us from producing a very precise number.

There will be a spreadsheet. There is always a spreadsheet.

Dave will sell 12,437 units in Year 1. Not 12,000. Don't be ridiculous.

12,437.

Eventually Dave arrives. He's the correct size. The correct material. Positano beach blue. His margin is considerably less alarming. He has brought 39,999 siblings.

His pink carton is whisked efficiently towards Warehouse 5, Bay 17.

The launch plan launches. The forecast forecasts. Dave is ready. The retailer takes another look at him. “Yeah, nah.”

And then something slightly inconvenient happens. The consumer sees Dave.

“BLOODY HELL. YES. DAVE. THAT'S EXACTLY WHAT I WANT.”

We will now require considerably more than 40,000 Dave's.
