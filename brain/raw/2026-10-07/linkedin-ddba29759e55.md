“More process prevents mistakes.”


Every serious incident, every missed commitment, every complaint from another department creates a temptation: add a step. Add a form. Add a checklist. Add an approval. “We’ll make sure this doesn’t happen again.”

On its own, this isn’t wrong. Some failures do reveal a missing safeguard. But over time, if you treat every problem as a process problem, you end up with something else: a slow, brittle system where people follow scripts instead of thinking.

More process often prevents movement, not mistakes.

Engineers learn to optimize for passing the process, not improving the outcome. They fill in templates because they have to, not because those templates help them reason better. Energy that could go into designing better systems gets redirected into satisfying the next layer of oversight.

The alternative is fewer steps and better systems.

Instead of asking, “What new rule will prevent this?” ask:

    “What expectation was unclear?”

    “What feedback loop was missing?”

    “What would make the right action the easiest one next time?”

Automated checks, solid observability, clear ownership, fast incident response rituals are systems improvements. They reduce mistakes without drowning the org in manual rituals. A small number of well-designed guardrails beats a forest of forms.
