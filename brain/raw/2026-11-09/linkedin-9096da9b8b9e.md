What Managing 250 People Taught Me About Leadership

At IBM, I was responsible for a national field organization of approximately 250 people.

50 full-time employees. Another 200 part-time representatives. Spread across the country.

And I learned pretty quickly that managing people and leading people are two entirely different things.

Managing is relatively straightforward.

You establish objectives, measure performance, review results, and hold people accountable.

Leadership? That’s where things get interesting.

Because people aren’t interchangeable.

What motivates one person may do absolutely nothing for another.

Some need direction. Others need encouragement. Some need a little more confidence in their abilities.

And some of your best performers simply need you to get the hell out of their way.

You can’t lead 250 people by treating them like 250 identical employees.

You have to understand what makes them tick.

I also learned that the person standing in front of the customer often knows something the person sitting in the executive office doesn’t.

And if you’re smart, you listen.

One of the mistakes leaders make is assuming that because they’re responsible for the organization, they’re supposed to have all the answers.

They’re not.

They’re supposed to build an organization capable of finding the answers.

Over the years, I’ve worked with some extraordinarily talented people.

I’ve also worked with people who needed more coaching, more patience, or simply someone willing to recognize potential they hadn’t yet recognized in themselves.

And there’s something particularly satisfying about watching someone develop into a stronger performer, a more confident professional, or eventually a leader themselves.

I’ve always believed that the success of a sales organization depends on more than having a few outstanding salespeople.

It depends on building a team where people understand the objectives, have the tools to succeed, and know their contributions matter.

That doesn’t mean lowering expectations.

Quite the opposite.

I believe in accountability. I believe in measurable results. And I believe people generally rise to the expectations you establish, provided you give them the support to get there.

But I also believe that leadership is about more than numbers on a spreadsheet.

A manager measures what people accomplish. A leader helps them accomplish more than they thought possible.

Twenty-five years later, I still believe that.

And I still believe the best leaders aren’t necessarily the smartest people in the room.

They’re the ones smart enough to recognize the talent around them.

What’s the most valuable leadership lesson you’ve learned from someone who reported to you?
