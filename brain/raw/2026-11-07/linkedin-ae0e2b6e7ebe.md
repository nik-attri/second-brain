Stop inventing vanity metrics to justify your AI investments.

If your AI project can’t be tied to a metric the CFO already tracks, it isn't an investment—it’s an experiment.

In my latest column for SiliconANGLE & theCUBE, I summarized a conversation with Colleen Aubrey, SVP of Applied AI Solutions at Amazon Web Services (AWS) and member of Amazon’s S-team, to discuss how enterprises can finally escape "AI pilot purgatory."

Her core message was to throw out the generic "AI ROI" slide. Don't measure "return on tokens" or prompt counts. Focus on real business impact.

Here are 5 key takeaways every IT and business leader needs to hear:
Feature parity is a trap.
Don’t try to replicate legacy ERPs or broad workflows out of the gate. Pick a sharp wedge where current tooling is weakest and people are drowning in signals (like Amazon did with voice quality in Connect, and now supply chain planners with Connect Decisions).
The metrics haven't changed.
Wells Vehicle Electronics used AWS AI to cut inventory by 7% in 3 months while expanding actively monitored SKUs from 20 to 2,000. They aren’t tracking tokens—they’re measuring working capital.
AI doesn’t remove bottlenecks; it moves them.
Amazon saw a 2x boost in deployment velocity not simply from coding assistants, but because the assistants exposed bad test coverage and weak deployment pipelines. Fix the downstream plumbing, or your output stays flat while activity spikes.
Own the outcome, not the task.
Task-based job descriptions will age poorly. As roles blend and tools evolve, teams must be evaluated on the business outcomes they drive, not the manual steps they execute.
Good AI with bad UX will fail.
Enterprises will reject AI that forces awkward workflows. Success comes from "humorphism"—designing AI that fits naturally into how people already work, picks up cues, and carries context forward.

The AI era won’t reward the companies running the most pilots. It will reward those that pick a painful problem, fix the underlying plumbing, and move real business metrics.
