EdTech founders, can we talk about how you introduce your product to schools?



Imagine walking into a school and saying:

"Our platform has automated attendance, fee management, result processing, parent communication and analytics."

Sounds impressive, right?

But the school owner is probably wondering:

"Okay... and what does that actually change for my school?"

This is where many product pitches lose people.

You know what your product can do. But the person you're pitching to needs to understand why those features matter to them.

Here are a few things worth trying:

1. Stop giving everyone the same pitch.

The teacher using your platform and the proprietor paying for it may have completely different priorities.

A teacher might care about spending less time recording attendance. A proprietor might care about having accurate attendance records without chasing multiple teachers for updates.

Speak to both.

2. Turn features into everyday situations.

Instead of saying "automated fee management", explain that the school can track outstanding payments without manually going through several notebooks or spreadsheets.

Help them picture the difference in their daily routine.

3. Don't just demo everything.

You don't have to show a school every feature your product has.

Start with one problem they actually mentioned. Show them how your product handles it.

A focused demonstration can be more useful than a 30-minute tour of every button.

4. Give your pilot a purpose.

A free trial shouldn't just be about getting a school to sign up.

Before it starts, agree on what success should look like.

Is it reducing the time spent preparing reports? Getting teachers to record attendance consistently? Making fee records easier to track?

Choose something you can actually observe and measure.

5. Ask one question founders sometimes forget:

"What would make this difficult for your staff to adopt?"

You might discover that the problem isn't the product itself. It could be internet access, staff training, existing routines or simply the time required to learn something new.

And that's useful information to have before you start onboarding an entire school.

Here's the thing:

A school doesn't buy your product simply because it has impressive features.

It needs to see how those features fit into its daily reality, solve a problem that matters and justify the investment.

Don't just show schools what your product can do. Help them see what could change when they use it.
