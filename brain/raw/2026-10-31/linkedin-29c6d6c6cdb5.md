What a waste! Weil, Gotshal & Manges LLP looks like it's collapsed - despite no debt, $1.2bn in revenue and a growing practice. How the hell did this happen, and what can we learn?

My own view is that this about the death of the 'traditional' professional service firm, and that what is currently happening in law, will soon happen in consulting. By a traditional consulting firm, I mean a partnership which pays its partners by what I would call a 'discretionary merit-based remuneration' i.e. a committee decides, rather than a spreadsheet, and reward is less about sales and more about mentoring, reputation buliding, and practice development.

The result of this compensation model is slower growth compared to competitors and frustrated ambitious 'rain-maker' partners. The result of this double-whammy is not only slower growth, but the eventual exit of the most valuable partners. 

Weil's revenue in 2025 was about 1.7 times its 2008 level, against roughly four to eight times at their competitors. Its profit per equity partner was $5.6m, against $8.6m to $11.1m at the firms now hiring its people. My friends at the firm say that profits went on partner compensation rather than recruitment and investment: the common 'jam today' problem that many partnerships face.

Profit is last year's number, and partners act on what they expect next year. A gap of $3m or more a partner to the firms bidding for them made the best rainmakers easy to move.

As with people, no firm is determined by its environment. What turned drift into collapse, I think, was how leadership handled what it could see on the horizon. The WSJ reports that Ramona Nee told the management committee in late August or September that she would not take the top job, and a source told me that partners learned only the weekend before it became public. 

So what can other PSFs learn from the fiasco? 

First, healthy profits does not mean that there is no problem if your revenue is based around key people who feel they are not receiving a fair share of compensation and decision-making. Compare partner earnings and growth each year with the firms that recruit your people, not with your own history.

When partner start to leave, act quickly, as this is akin to a 'run on the bank': other partners will be concerned that future profits will fall and, at worst, they will be bailing out the firm rather than paying for that third home in Cornwall. If the the blocker to change is one person, remove them quickly, even if they are a rain-maker - the firm must come first.

The market and the industry has changed. Firms can no longer rely on partner loyalty, a family culture, or excessive partner extraction of profits. Balancing long-term investment for a growing firm against short-term calls on capital is tricky, but the partnership model, in my view, is not dead. It can be suited to the investment requirements of the modern era and the more flighty personalities of partners, but it takes careful balancing.
