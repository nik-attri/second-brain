10 Things QAD Consultants Wish Their Clients Knew — Post 6 of 10
"We can't automate a broken process."
This is the sentence clients least want to hear—and most need to.
Because automation is seductive. It promises speed, accuracy, fewer people, fewer errors. And QAD can automate a great deal: backflushing, MRP, scheduling, approvals, costing, invoicing.
But automation does not fix a process. It accelerates it.
If the process is sound, automation makes it faster and more reliable.
If the process is broken, automation makes it fail faster, with more expensive consequences.
Why This Matters
A manual process fails in front of the person doing it. They see the problem, they correct it, they move on.
An automated process fails silently—and propagates.
A wrong ship-to on the customer master flows through automated picking and dispatch. The goods arrive at the wrong plant—and nobody notices until the customer calls.
Manual errors are visible. Automated errors are systemic.
Because automation removes the last human checkpoint—the moment when someone would have seen the error before it became an event outside the company.
What Goes Wrong
Nobody maps the process before automating it. The team configures the transaction, but never asks how the work actually flows, who approves, or what happens when it goes wrong.
The process was never standard to begin with. Three plants do it three ways. Automation freezes one version and breaks the other two. 
The Deeper Problem
Clients often ask for automation to solve a symptom: too much manual work, too many errors, too slow.
But the symptom is usually caused by a process problem—unclear ownership, missing standards, uncontrolled exceptions, or no feedback loop.
The question is never "Can we automate this?"
It is "Is this process worth automating—as it currently works?"
If the answer is no, the consultant's job is to say so. Automating a broken process is not an improvement.
It is a commitment.
How to Avoid It
1. Map the process before configuring it. As-is, then to-be. Include exceptions, approvals, and failure paths—not just the happy path.
2. Prove the process manually first. Run it by hand through one full cycle. If it doesn't work smoothly by hand, it won't work automated.
3. Start with high-volume, low-variance processes. Automate the predictable. Handle the variable with judgment.
For Clients:
Fix the process first. Standardize it. Assign ownership. Then automate—and automate deliberately.
For Consultants:
Say the harder thing early: "Let's fix the process first."
Your client may resist today. They will thank you in eighteen months.  
The Bottom Line:
Automation does not fix a broken process. It scales it.

Think About It:
If you're a client—is the process you're asking us to automate the same one you'd design from scratch today?
If you're a consultant—when did you last challenge an automation request before configuring it?
