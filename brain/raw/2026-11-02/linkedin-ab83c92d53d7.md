Gen Z Isn't Unwilling to Work. They're Unwilling to Do Meaningless Work.

I keep hearing managers say young employees don't want to work. In most teams I've seen, that's not quite true.

What Gen Z resists is not work itself. It's work where the process matters more than the outcome.

Too often, managers give steps instead of goals. Do it this way, fill in this form, copy this data, follow this exact order. Gen Z employees ask why, not because they want to rebel, but because they grew up with search, automation, and tools that show them a shorter path. 

When you insist on the old steps, you waste their best skill: finding a more effective way.

Give them a goal, and the story often changes. 

For example, instead of saying "spend three hours manually compiling this weekly report sheet by sheet," say "by Friday, we need a report leadership can act on in five minutes."

Given that goal, a young team member may build a simple template or automation, cut the work from three hours to thirty minutes, and improve accuracy at the same time. Same goal, better method.

If the manager then says "no, do it the old way," the employee doesn't just lose time. They learn that initiative will be corrected, not rewarded. That's when disengagement starts, and eventually, resignation follows. Not because they were lazy, but because their talent had nowhere to go.

If you want to keep Gen Z talent, try three shifts:
1. Give the goal and the standard of good work, not just the steps. What does success look like, by when, and to what quality bar?
2. Set guardrails, then allow choice of method. Be clear about what must not change, such as compliance or a client promise, and leave the rest open.
3. Reward a better method when it works. Adopt it, credit the person, and make it the new standard.

So here's a question for managers: 
The last time someone on your team found a faster way, did you adopt it, or did you correct them back to the old way? 

Your answer may explain more about retention than any salary survey will.
