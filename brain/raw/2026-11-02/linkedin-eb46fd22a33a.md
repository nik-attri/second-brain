Design for simplicity, not for image!

Simplicity in design is everything; simplicity is about the user, not the designer. And yet, I increasingly see more colour, flashy icons, and workflows built around screen design rather than the user. And for me, this is the wrong approach.

Simplicity by design means taking a complex problem and designing a workflow that looks boring and simple, as if it required little design thought. The complexity behind the design should be invisible to the user.

An Excel spreadsheet is a brilliant design: just rows, columns, numbers and formulas, yet incredibly complex underneath. Try doing manually what Excel does. Its basic design has changed little in decades, because it works. You don’t need to understand everything Excel can do to start using it.

Counter that with the new Outlook interface. Pretty much everyone I know sticks with the legacy design, the look and feel that they’ve always known. Not simply because it’s familiar, but because it works for them. Changing how something looks doesn’t necessarily make it better.

And yet, an awful lot of modern software design is overly complex by the time it arrives with users. Sure, it looks flashy, the icons are cool, the colours bold, but the core process is just….yeuch.

As a dyslexic, I used to write far too much; I’m better now. I’ve learnt that getting a message across often means taking things out, not adding more. Software engineering design often feels similarly overcomplicated and not user-centric.

When demonstrating our software, the best feedback is, ‘this is so simple’ or ‘it’s not as complex as I thought’. That’s what we strive for. People ask why we design for simplicity: we’re not the user. We build processes and workflows for people less comfortable with computers than we are.

And that’s the point. We might understand the technology, the processes and everything happening behind the scenes, but why should the user need to?

If you’re working to produce great software for your users, how simple is the workflow? Have you, or your target users, agreed that it’s as simple as possible? If you decided, then you might be building what you want, not what they need.

Fancy icons and clever colour schemes matter; the look and feel matter, but a great-looking piece of software that maintains or increases process complexity is still a bad design.

If you’re asking a question and need an answer, make it obvious and simple.

Simplicity by design, not by accident.
