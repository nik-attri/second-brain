This week there has been yet more news of charities  facing significant structural change, restructuring and redeployment. Roles are disappearing, teams are changing, and people are having to prove their value in an increasingly uncertain landscape.

But as heavy as it feels to be in this right now, there’s massive opportunity.

What if instead of simply moving people around the org. chart, we used this moment to rethink what good leadership actually looks like? 

This is what got me thinking about the Peter Principle. People get promoted to the point where they’re no longer effective, and somehow, they stay there. Stuck.  What we really need to talk about though, is what happens to the people stuck behind stuck people. 

Teams know when leadership isn't working. They notice inconsistencies, a lack of accountability and weak strategy. Often, the most capable people quietly compensate for the shortcomings around them until frustration, exhaustion and resentment take their toll. Burnout runs rampant.

Organisations often end up protecting people in roles that don't work for them or the  organisation, while overlooking the talent, potential and leadership capability sitting right under our noses.

The opposite does already exist in our organisations. I know because I see it in action every day. My current line manager is the best example I could ask for. He's someone I respect, trust and, frankly, would run into oncoming traffic for. (Please don't tell him that. He'll get ideas.)

I genuinely believe he wants this organisation to be the best it can possibly be. 

My manager understands something fundamental that so many others miss - the only way to build the best organisation is to be the best it can be with and for its people. That means recognising potential, supporting development, making difficult decisions when necessary and understanding that people aren't simply resources to be moved around a spreadsheet.

It's the difference between wanting people to prove their worth to an organisation and recognising that an organisation has a responsibility to help its people thrive.

Of course, not every struggling manager is in the wrong role. Sometimes people are promoted without the training, support or preparation they need. People deserve a genuine opportunity to develop, and support and accountability must go hand in hand.

As roles change and organisations restructure, we can rethink how we identify talent, develop leaders and create the conditions for people to do their best work. We can stop assuming that being good at one job automatically makes someone good at managing others. We can recognise that leadership is a skill, not simply a reward for being good at your previous job. And we can be brave enough to ask whether we're putting the right people in the right roles, for the right reasons.
