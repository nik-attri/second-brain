The most useful thing I've done in the last 12 months isn't building the tool. It's telling people why I didn't build what they asked for.

I've had a group of project managers testing something I've been putting together in evenings and weekends. The feedback has been blunt, which is exactly what I wanted. Some of it I've acted on within a week. The RAG status being manually set rather than calculated, the missing backup reminders, the error messages that told you nothing useful, all fixed because they were plainly right. Other requests I haven't built, and rather than quietly ignoring them and hoping nobody noticed, I went back and explained the reasoning. Browser only storage stays as it is because the security trade off matters more than the convenience. Certain features aren't coming yet because they'd add complexity before the core is solid.

What's struck me is how unusual people find that second part. One tester said being told exactly why something wasn't built was rarer than it should be, and they're right. We do the same thing in delivery all the time. A stakeholder asks for something, we can't or shouldn't do it, so we let it drift, bury it in a backlog, or agree to it vaguely and hope it goes away. The honest no with a clear reason behind it is harder in the moment and better for the relationship every single time. People rarely resent the decision. They resent being managed around it. When did you last tell a stakeholder no properly, and how did it actually go?
