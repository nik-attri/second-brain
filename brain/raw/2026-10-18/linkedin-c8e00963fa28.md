“That is not possible.”

People around me believed that about automating some tasks in our department.

I did not.

Not because I knew how to do it.

But I kept thinking: if anyone can automate repetitive tasks, why should this one be any different?

So, I kept trying.

A lesson from a workshop I attended in Thailand had stayed with me:

Identify the task that consumes the most time in your business. There is a good chance that solving it will make a meaningful difference.

I started looking at my own work through that lens.

Could our work be made easier?

Could documents that we prepared manually be generated with a single click?

Could reports that took hours be processed in around 20 minutes using Python?

I started experimenting with Claude, HTML, scripts, and Python. I identified problems, built solutions, tested them, and refined them along the way.

Then came the student performance reports.

Preparing one report could take anywhere from 30 minutes to an hour. It was a process where manual errors could occur.

My initial approach, which had worked for other tasks, was not working here.

I had to rethink the process and try a different approach.

There were frustrating moments. Claude would sometimes produce incorrect solutions, and running out of usage limits meant waiting. Meanwhile, my regular responsibilities, academics, and community commitments were not going to pause.

So I kept the work moving, returned to manual tasks when necessary, and continued building whenever I could.

Eventually, I developed a workflow that brought report preparation down to under five minutes per student, followed by the necessary checks.

But the moment that stayed with me was not seeing the solution work.

It was seeing my colleagues' reaction.

People who had been dealing with this tedious task could finally breathe a little easier. I walked them through the process one by one, listened to their feedback, and made corrections as issues came up.

The workflow is now in use.

That experience taught me three things:

→ You don't need to know everything before you start. You need to understand the problem well enough to begin learning how to solve it.

→ When one approach fails, reconsider the approach—not necessarily the goal. Sometimes the solution requires a completely different way of thinking.

→ An improvement matters most when other people can actually use it. Building something is one part of the job. Making it practical, reliable, and useful to your team is another.

I am still learning. I am not a software engineer, and I do not claim to be one.

I am a business professional learning to use technology to solve business problems.

And I am beginning to believe that one of the most valuable questions we can ask at work is surprisingly simple:

“Why are we still doing this manually?”

Sometimes, that is where a better way of working begins.
