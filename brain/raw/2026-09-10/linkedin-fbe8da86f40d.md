What Should My Child Learn to Be Relevant in the Job Market 10–15 Years From Now? (An old post from 2 years ago (AI scared many people about learning and unemployment perspective and was partially false) . My thoughts follow)
This is one of the most common questions I hear, especially from parents.
A little history
Modern school, in the form we know today — with discipline, schedules, subjects, classrooms, etc. — was largely reinvented about 300 years ago, following the Prussian model.
The original goal wasn’t necessarily to create creative and independent people, but, among other things, disciplined and obedient ones, suited to the structures of the state and society of that time.
In short: the school system was designed to produce trained soldiers. Well-trained cannon fodder. LOL
Today, things are somewhat different.
Many young people leave school with skills that are not fully aligned with the job market and then have to be trained again by companies and corporations, directly on the job.
Now, let's get back to education in the age of the AI revolution.
The problem is that many teachers — and many parents — struggle to adapt to rapid technological change and tend to reject new technologies from the start.
A good parallel is the pocket calculator. (AI somehow is also a very powerful pocket calc. btw)
When pocket calculators first became popular, children weren’t allowed to use them at school.
The reasoning?
“You need to do the calculations in your head.”
And some parents thought:
“The child is becoming lazy. They need to learn the hard way, not take the easy path.”
OK. 🙂
But how do we look at pocket calculators today?
How many of us can calculate, in our heads: log₈(5)?
And more importantly, why should we spend our time and mental energy performing calculations manually when a tool can do them instantly and accurately?
I’m not saying mathematics isn’t important.
Quite the opposite.
You need to understand what you’re calculating, why you’re calculating it, and how to interpret the result.
That’s the difference.
The same thing is happening now with AI.
Kids tend to adapt relatively naturally to new technology.
But what about adults?
What should a child learn?
In my opinion, rather than memorizing a rigid list of skills that are “in demand now,” a child should become flexible.
They should develop:
a broad general culture;
the ability to learn new things quickly;
critical thinking;
creativity;
the ability to ask good questions;
the capacity to work with AI, rather than compete with it;
and, perhaps most importantly, curiosity.
Because nobody really knows what the job market will look like in 10–15 years.
But we can be pretty sure of one thing:
It will be different from today.
That’s why, for children — and for adults — there must be continuous learning.
Not just a defined period called “school.”
Not just university.
Not just one course or certification.
Learning should become a permanent operating system.
