Yesterday I joined a panel at Flux Convergence called “Whose Job Is It Anyway? The Secret to Managing Cross-Functional Chaos.” It was a conversation about what happens as AI makes it easier for people to build, analyse, automate and create beyond the traditional boundaries of their roles.

One of the things I raised was focus. AI is making many forms of execution more abundant, but human attention is not. There is also a lot of FOMO in organisations right now. Another model, another tool, another workflow to automate, another capability we should apparently already be using.

More capacity gives us more things we could do. It does not tell us which ones deserve our attention. That is why I think we need to be more careful about what we count as progress.

We talk a lot about the velocity AI gives us. I think we need to care much more about the velocity of user value.

The conversation about blurred roles was interesting too. I actually think some of that blurring is healthy. Product, Engineering, Design, Research and Analytics still bring different expertise, but good cross-functional work has never depended on everyone staying neatly in their lane.

An engineer can ask a question that exposes an unclear outcome. A designer or researcher can challenge an assumption that has become accepted as fact. Someone in Analytics can question whether the thing we are measuring actually tells us what we think it does. Those questions do not have to belong exclusively to one function.

The expertise can stay specialised. The questions don’t have to.

That matters even more when AI allows one person to take an idea much further on their own. Being able to build something independently is powerful, but I don’t think we should confuse independence with better quality. Some of the value of working cross-functionally comes from having other people challenge the idea, expose our blind spots and help turn the individual parts into a better whole.

Then, in a breakout after the panel, someone asked a great question: Why don’t we stop and remove things?

It is such a useful thing to unpack because if AI saves us time, why is our instinct so often to fill the space again? More features, more experiments, more output, more things competing for the same finite attention.

Why not use some of that capacity to simplify? Remove a process that no longer earns its place. Retire something we are still maintaining mostly because it already exists. Do fewer things properly. Or simply choose not to refill every bit of capacity we create.

AI productivity should create permission to remove, not an obligation to produce.

Sometimes the right answer will absolutely be more. But I think there is something important in treating new capacity as a choice, rather than automatically turning it back into more output.

Thank you to Danielle Macdonald and Amy Kelly for creating the space, and to my fellow panellists Helen Durrant and Hazal Muhtar for such a thoughtful conversation.
