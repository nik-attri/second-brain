Another Posterior SI Fusion Implant: Innovation or Added Complexity?

I’ve heard another posterior SI fusion system may be approaching the market. Before celebrating, examine the procedure, evidence, and commercial strategy.

First, consider implant delivery.

Suppose a specialized cannula is used to guide and advance an implant into a prepared SI joint. As resistance increases, the advancement force must be balanced by an opposing reaction force.

If the surgeon must provide that counterforce manually, what happens while simultaneously controlling implant advancement, maintaining trajectory, and stabilizing the instrumentation?

Could resistance shift the cannula or compromise positioning? How repeatable is the technique across surgeons?

A system may manage these forces. If so, show the validation. The issue is reproducibility, not one successful insertion.

Second, consider open visualization.

Direct visualization and deliberate joint preparation may offer advantages. They also involve exposure, anatomical identification, and surgical competencies that cannot be assumed from experience with image-guided injections alone.

That raises a commercialization question: If the procedure requires open visualization and surgical preparation, why would pain management be the first target market rather than experienced spine surgeons?

This is not an argument against pain physicians. Appropriately trained and credentialed physicians should be evaluated on competency, not specialty labels. But the clinical demands of the procedure should determine the training pathway—not the size of the sales opportunity.

Third, fixation is not fusion.

Implant stability, distraction, and early pain relief do not by themselves demonstrate biological arthrodesis. How are opposing articular surfaces prepared? Where can bridging bone form? What do independent CT assessments show at meaningful follow-up?

And when clinical data is cited, which exact implant and approach were studied? How many patients? In what countries? At what follow-up intervals? 

A registered study is a plan to collect evidence—not a completed outcome. Data from one implant should not automatically be transferred to another.

Finally, more distributors do not necessarily mean more cases—or better results.

A distributor can introduce a technology. They cannot substitute for mechanical validation, surgical reproducibility, credible fusion evidence, or sustained physician adoption.

You cannot distribute your way around an unanswered clinical question.

The next meaningful advance in SI fusion won’t be defined by another screw, another cannula, or another distributor agreement.

It will be defined by a procedure that is mechanically sound, appropriately taught, biologically credible, and supported by outcomes that withstand scrutiny.

We’re not simply placing implants. We’re trying to achieve durable arthrodesis.
