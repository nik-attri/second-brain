The most important use of AI in medical technology may not be an “AI device” at all.

It may be a better implant, a more promising molecule, a smarter clinical trial or an earlier safety signal—created because AI helped us learn faster.
We are already seeing glimpses of this.

Rentosertib, an investigational treatment for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, progressed to a randomised Phase 2a trial after AI was used to identify its biological target and generate the molecule. The study involved 71 patients. This does not prove that AI makes drugs successful—but it demonstrates that AI-supported discovery can produce candidates capable of reaching rigorous human trials. [1]

In surgery, a recent prospective study of 48 patients combined AI-assisted CT planning with patient-specific instrumentation for unicompartmental knee replacement. It improved implant positioning, tibial coverage and resection accuracy. Importantly, short-term patient outcomes were not significantly different—an essential reminder that technical precision must ultimately translate into meaningful clinical benefit. [2]

AI may also change what happens after a device enters clinical practice.
Researchers analysing the records of 6,583 hip-replacement patients used machine learning to extract implant, pain and complication data from clinical notes. Their system increased patient coverage compared with a manually curated registry and demonstrated the potential for scalable, near-real-time device surveillance. [3]

This is where I believe the real opportunity lies.

AI can support the entire medical-technology lifecycle:
Design → simulation → patient selection → implantation → outcome monitoring → redesign.
It could help us test implant geometries and materials before manufacturing, identify which patients are most likely to benefit, improve procedural planning, sharpen clinical trials and detect safety signals earlier.

None of this removes the need for prospective validation, transparent governance, regulatory oversight or experienced clinical judgement.

AI can accelerate good ideas—but it can also accelerate poor assumptions.
The future may not be an operating theatre filled with devices labelled “AI-powered”. It may be a learning medical-technology ecosystem in which every procedure and outcome helps make the next generation of treatments safer, more precise and more effective.

Where do you see the greatest near-term opportunity: discovery, device design, clinical trials or post-market surveillance?
