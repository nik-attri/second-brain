New academic year, same reminder: access matters.

As students return to schools, colleges and universities, this feels like an important reminder.

A student struggling does not necessarily mean a student who is incapable, unmotivated, disengaged or “not trying hard enough”. Sometimes, the environment is creating or merely amplifying their struggle.

ADHD, autism, dyslexia, anxiety and other non-visible disabilities can affect executive functioning, processing, concentration, communication, sensory regulation, organisation and the ability to demonstrate knowledge in the ways education traditionally expects.

Reasonable adjustments are not about giving someone an unfair advantage; they work to remove the unnecessary barriers to learning that most have had to endure for too long. For some, completely destroying their association with education, learning, and academic success. 

So, this academic year, here are a few things we can all do differently:

Lecturers & educators: Be explicit. Give clear written instructions alongside verbal ones. Break larger tasks into manageable stages. Avoid assuming that missed deadlines, lateness, silence or difficulty initiating work automatically mean a lack of commitment. Ask, “What might help you access this?” rather than, “Why haven’t you done this?”

Students: Learn what helps your brain learn. Ask what reasonable adjustments are available and use the adjustments that are available to you. Ask for clarification when instructions are ambiguous. Use assistive technology, reminders, visual planning, body doubling and academic support where helpful. You do not have to wait until you are drowning before asking for support.

Parents & carers: Look beyond behaviour. Procrastination, avoidance, emotional overwhelm, shutdown and apparent disorganisation can sometimes tell us that the demands being placed upon a young person exceed their current capacity or available support. Curiosity will often tell us more than criticism.

Most importantly, reasonable adjustments still require individualisation. Two students with ADHD, autism or dyslexia may need completely different things. A diagnosis tells us something about a person; it does not give us an instruction manual.

So, for the 2026/27 academic year, perhaps one question belongs in every learning environment:

“What does this person need in order to have a fair opportunity to succeed?”

Inclusion is not simply allowing somebody into the room. We’ve seen what happens when someone is just ‘let in the room’ without ensuring the room is safe, welcoming and supportive. True equality and inclusion is considering whether the room was designed in a way that allows them to participate once they get there.

Here’s to a more curious, compassionate and genuinely inclusive academic year.
