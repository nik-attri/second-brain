We recently purchased retail space that will become new healthcare clinics.

Right now, it’s mostly empty.

Soon, providers need to walk through those doors and take care of patients.

An empty building does not become a functioning clinic because somebody added an opening date to a spreadsheet.

There’s a lot that has to happen between those two things.

And honestly, this is the kind of problem I love.

Give me the outcome and let me work backward.

What does opening day need to look like?

Not from IT’s perspective.

From the perspective of the people trying to do their jobs.

They need to sit down, log in, access their systems, make calls, print, use clinical equipment and take care of patients.

That’s the outcome.

Now comes the fun part.

How the hell do we get there?

What has to happen first?

What can happen at the same time?

Who is waiting on whom?

What assumptions are we making?

And the question that gets increasingly uncomfortable as the deadline approaches:

What are we forgetting?

My background in customer experience shapes how I approach these problems because there is always a customer.

Here, it might be a provider trying to get through a packed day without technology making it harder.

Ultimately, there is a patient on the other side of all of it.

Different customer.

Same question.

What are they trying to accomplish, and what are we doing to make that easier?

That’s why I don’t see this as an IT project.

Technology is part of it.

So are facilities, operations, clinical leadership, vendors and a pile of dependencies with no respect for the organizational chart.

Neither does the deadline.

That’s where leadership matters.

Get the right people talking early.

Find the dependencies.

Challenge assumptions.

Make ownership clear.

Find the problem while it is still small and inexpensive to fix.

Because the problem you’re handed isn’t always the problem you actually have.

The technology problem might be a process problem.

The process problem might be a communication problem.

The communication problem might be an ownership problem.

Sometimes everybody is solving exactly what they were asked to solve, and the pieces still don’t fit together.

That’s when you return to the beginning.

Who is the customer?

What does success look like for them?

Then work backward.

If we do this right, opening day won’t look impressive.

People will walk in.

They’ll log in.

The phones will work.

The systems will be ready.

Providers will take care of patients.

Almost nobody will know how many decisions, dependencies and “Well, that’s interesting” moments made it possible.

Good.

They shouldn’t have to.

That is what success looks like.
