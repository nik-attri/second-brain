While I am #running my first SPAR 30

Hungary has opened a larger market for battery flexibility. 

Now we need to see whether it produces bankable revenue.

On 5 October, #MAVIR completed Hungary’s connection to #MARI, the European platform for manually activated frequency-restoration reserves. Márton Gábor Kádár 

This followed the #PICASSO connection for automatic reserves on 1 October.
Roy van den Kieboom

For Hungarian battery storage, this is a meaningful operational change.

A qualified resource can now participate in balancing-energy activation within a wider European market. The battery’s value is therefore no longer limited to buying electricity cheaply and selling it later.

It may also be paid for responding when the electricity system needs flexibility.

This reinforces the question I raised in my previous BESS post:

Will storage regulation classify batteries mainly by what they are—or by what they do?

The European balancing framework increasingly focuses on the second question.

But access to a larger market should not be confused with a bankable business model.

A lender still needs evidence of activation frequency, expected prices, available capacity payments, degradation costs and the ability to combine balancing revenues with other services.

PICASSO and MARI create the market infrastructure. They do not create a revenue floor.

The practical next step should therefore be transparent post-launch data showing how Hungarian balancing prices, activation volumes and participation by storage develop.

Only then can investors assess whether European market integration has improved the financing case for Hungarian BESS—or merely widened the arena in which revenue remains uncertain.
