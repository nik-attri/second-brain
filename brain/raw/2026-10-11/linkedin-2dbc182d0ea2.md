The Age of AI Attacks: A Lesson from Trafalgar
"It's the same old attacks, just with AI. Why all the fuss?"
Some say this about ARTEX. Others see the dawn of routine, large-scale hacking. I agree, and the reason lies at sea, 200 years ago.

ARTEX is an autonomous AI pentesting agent: give it a target, and it maps assets, finds flaws, and runs the tools it needs. Since late September, it has surfaced in attacks on Korean financial firms. The source is now closed, but released code can't be recalled.
Napoleon conquered Europe with artillery and speed, but his fate was sealed at sea, by seeds sown centuries earlier in the metal of a cannon.

Cannon were long cast in bronze, and costly copper and tin made every gun precious. In 1543, England mastered casting them in iron. Iron melts near 1,200°C versus about 1,000°C for bronze, demanding bigger furnaces and harder skills. The payoff: guns at a third of the cost, on ship after ship. A rare weapon became a common one.

Britain then built the carronade, a short, hard-hitting iron gun. France copied it late, in bronze, switching to iron only in 1804. In March 1805, Napoleon admitted they had "done us great harm everywhere."

At Trafalgar that October, the Franco-Spanish fleet had more ships and heavier guns, yet lost 18 of 33 ships of the line. Britain lost none. The edge was speed: drilled British crews fired two or three times for every enemy shot.

ARTEX tells the same story. AI didn't invent a new cannon. Like cast iron, it makes an expert weapon cheap and common, and it fires faster. Calling it "just AI on old attacks" is like saying Britain "just fired faster." Trafalgar shows what that "just" means.

Hacking used to be vertical: a few experts digging deep into one target. AI agents automate and generalize that work, so one attacker can hit many targets at once. This attacker reportedly hit several institutions in about two weeks, and hundreds of ARTEX-linked servers are visible online.

Our defenses assume the opposite: protect the crown jewels, guard network zones. AI breaks both premises.
When every asset is attacked at once, nothing is low priority. The weakest point opens first.
AI links technical flaws with business context (workflows, permissions), building attack paths that ignore network boundaries.
Reported entry points: a loan-broker status service and an employee mobile app. Hardly crown jewels.
The answer is universal, AI-based defense: every asset covered, at machine speed. Cast iron put guns on every ship; AI must put defense on every asset. A few precious bronze guns cannot guard the sea.
Britain's speed came from training, not just guns. AI alone won't win; it must learn from real incident response. Korea, among the world's most targeted nations, has years of it: our greatest asset.
The age of AI attacks has begun. One question remains: whose speed will we fight at?
