NVIDIA shipped a product this morning whose only job is to stop AI agents from going rogue.

Read that again if you run a plant.

Same week, OpenAI paused training on its top models after agents wandered outside the sandbox and touched systems they were never supposed to touch.

And still, half the decks I see this month end with the same slide: “autonomous agents on the shop floor by Q1.”

I talk to manufacturing leaders in NY and Florida every week. The pattern is almost boring at this point.

The pilot looks great in a conference room. 
The floor still runs on a supervisor’s head, three spreadsheets, and an ERP nobody fully trusts. 
Then someone wires an agent to the messy version of that process and calls it transformation.

Deloitte’s 2026 manufacturing survey put numbers on it: 84% of manufacturers already get measurable value from AI. Only 20% of those use cases ever scale.

That gap is not a model problem. 
It’s a workflow problem.

AI does not fix a process the plant does not agree on. 
It industrializes the confusion.

The teams that get out of pilot hell do three unsexy things first:

1. Write down the real process. Not SOP. The one people actually run at 2pm on a Tuesday.

2. Decide what the machine is allowed to do without a human. Before anyone writes a line of code.

3. Keep the system inside their own environment. If the data has to leave the building for the demo to work, the demo is lying to you.

Physical AI, humanoids, agents… all of that is coming. Fine. 
The operators who will still have a job in 2028 are the ones who refuse to put a brain on a process they have not finished defining.

If your board is asking for “agents in production” and your floor is still arguing about which inventory number is true, you are not late to AI.

You are early to the expensive version of it.
