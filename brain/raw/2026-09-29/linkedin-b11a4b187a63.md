"You're doing the same crappy work as everyone else, but somehow yours is the only team that's happy doing it."

A manager said that to my team, years ago. He wasn't wrong about the work.

An innovation project had just been shut down, and I'd rejoined a team buried in bug fixes. Every client ran their own branch of the product, quality complaints kept piling up, and engineers were tired of fixing the same category of problem again and again.

I told the team plainly: we were going to be the most innovative team in the program.

Test automation was still rare in what we did at the time, and that gap became the target. Instead of another round of manual regression testing, we cut old code, replaced sprawling if-else logic with proper design patterns, and let the tests we built along the way double as proof that nothing had broken.

Same category of work as everyone else. Different way of doing it.

There was one rule above the rest: quality first.

It's one of the few times I lost my temper as a manager, when someone pushed a change to production without testing it. That one shortcut could have undone the only thing separating us from every other team drowning in the same backlog.

Around the same time, every other team doing similar bug-fixing work got rolled back to an earlier, more stable release. Ours didn't.

The regression tests gave the product manager enough confidence to keep shipping our changes, and that trust bought us the room to keep doing it our way instead of being folded back into everyone else's process.

Looking back, three things were doing the work at once: a goal we'd chosen for ourselves, not been handed; a visibly better way of doing the job, not just more of it; and the room to keep doing it our way, earned by not breaking anything along the way. Take any one of those away and it's just bug-fixing.

The first and the third still hold up today. A team can still choose its own goal, and it can still earn the room to work its own way by not breaking things. It's the middle one I keep getting stuck on, and it keeps resurfacing whenever I look at the tools teams use now. Back then, a better way of doing the job meant cutting old code by hand and choosing the right pattern for it. An agent can now produce something that looks like that in seconds.

So what counts as mastery now, when the part that used to take years to build can be generated on demand?
