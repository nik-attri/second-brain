Three customers pay your entire salary. The other forty are noise.

You know their names. You know their payment schedules. You know which one delayed last quarter and which one threatens to switch if your price moves 2%.

This is not a growth problem. This is a hostage situation you've built yourself.

I've walked into thirty-two factories in the last five years where a ₹26 Cr annual turnover business was entirely dependent on two large OEMs. The owner had trained engineers, added shifts, installed new tooling — all to service one buyer's capacity. When that buyer's sourcing team decided to "consolidate," or when their own customer cancelled an order, the factory ran at 31% utilization the next quarter.

The machinery didn't break. Demand didn't vanish. One person's email did.

What makes this worse is the isolation. You can't tell your banker — he'll see concentration risk and tighten your limits. You can't tell your family — they'll think the business is fragile. You can't even tell the second-tier customers you're trying to grow, because they sense desperation and use it to negotiate margin down another 3%.

So you sit alone with the spreadsheet every month, calculating how many days of runway you have if the largest customer cuts orders by 40%.

How many of your top-five customers could actually disappear and still leave you breaking even, without a restructure?

Manufacturing owners: I spend a quarter of my time on exactly this — mapping customer concentration and the specific sequence of price, cost, and lead time decisions that got you here, not where to find new customers tomorrow. Every week I work through one real factory's profit-leak using this lens.

P.S. The ones who survive this are not the ones who added more customers fastest — they're the ones who raised prices to the 60% that matter, and let the noise go.
