I spent part of last weekend unplugging a little, getting back into nature, and touching some sky. Coming back means catching up on everything I deliberately ignored while I was gone. 

While working through LinkedIn, I came across a post from Steve Gompertz that stuck with me. Steve was calling attention to anonymous, AI-heavy Quality accounts publishing questionable or recycled material — sometimes even selling SOPs and templates — with no identifiable practitioner, organization, or real accountability behind the work.

It got me thinking… 
For the record, I’m not anti-AI. Not even close. I use it extensively.

But there’s a difference between using AI as a tool and outsourcing professional judgment to it. Someone still has to understand the problem. Challenge the output. Apply experience. Iterate. And ultimately be willing to put their name behind the result.

But Steve’s post collided with another thought that’s been rattling around in my head. Quality, Continuous Improvement, and Operational Excellence practitioners build useful things all the time. Diagnostics. Worksheets. Decision trees. Assessment tools. Templates. Frameworks. And those little spreadsheets that somehow become indispensable. We’re actually pretty damn good at version control when something becomes part of the formal system — SOPs, policies, controlled forms, specifications, and so on.

Our own practitioner tools? Not so much.
• They tend to live on a hard drive somewhere.
• They get emailed to someone. Copied. Modified. Carried to another organization. Maybe turned into a PDF.
• And occasionally, somewhere along the way, declared a “best practice.”

That’s when I had one of those: How the hell have I never connected these dots before?

I’ve spent years participating in open-source communities around my own interests and hobbies. I’ve shared things. Used what other people built. Modified them. Occasionally broken them. Improved them. Learned from people who figured something out long before I did. I know this model.

Build it. Share it. Fork it. Change it. Test it. Improve it.

And, when you’ve made it better, maybe contribute something back so the next person doesn’t have to start from scratch. 

Yet somehow, after all those years, I’d never really connected that mindset to the professional tools we build. 

Which left me with a pretty obvious question:

Why don’t we do more of that?
We’re the Continuous Improvement people, after all.
