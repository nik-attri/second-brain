AI will not just automate the enterprise. It will challenge the organizational logic on which the modern enterprise was built.
For decades, we optimized companies around two constraints: human cognition was expensive, and responsibility had to be distributed.
That gave us outsourcing, management layers, steering committees, approval chains, status reporting, and armies of people translating information between disconnected systems.
Agentic AI changes the economics behind those structures.
When execution becomes cheap, context becomes the asset.
When policy can be enforced continuously, approvals become less valuable.
When organizational memory becomes machine-queryable, tribal knowledge loses its monopoly.
When thousands of decisions can be evaluated automatically, human rubber-stamping stops being meaningful governance.
But there is a much harder problem hiding underneath:
What happens to accountability when there is no longer a human “throat to choke”?
The AI-native enterprise therefore cannot simply remove humans from workflows. It needs a different operating model—one built around provenance, evaluation harnesses, explicit execution boundaries, telemetry, institutional memory, and accountable system ownership.
My new article explores why this transition could reshape not only software delivery, but also outsourcing economics, management hierarchies, corporate memory, governance, and the meaning of responsibility itself.
The interesting question is no longer:
“How can we use AI inside our existing organization?”
It is:
“If AI existed when we designed the organization, would we have built it this way at all?”
