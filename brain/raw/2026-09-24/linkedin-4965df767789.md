It all started with a (cancelled) tennis match.

A few months ago, I was supposed to play tennis with a friend who runs a small electrical wholesale company.

Unfortunately, about an hour before we were due to start, he called and cancelled.
"He still has too much work tonight."

It was Friday evening, so I told him: “C’mon, it can wait until Monday. Let's play!”

But no chance.

An hour later, I ordered two pizzas grabbed some drinks and drove over to his company.
I mean, pizza and drinks come close to playing tennis.

So we sat there and ate pizza. 

At some point I was curious and asked him what was so important that the owner of the company had to spend his Friday evening doing it personally.
No one else was there. Just him.

So he explained:
One of his employees had been off sick that day. She was the person who normally processed the incoming orders. Since she couldn’t do it, he had to jump in.

Okay, that made me even more curious.
“What does processing incoming orders actually mean?”

So he showed me.
Most of their orders arrive by email.
Someone has to open each email, find the relevant information and manually enter it into the order-management system. It takes around two or three minutes per order.

Slightly shocked, I asked him: “How many orders do you receive every day?”

Completely calmly, he said: “Hmm, like around 50 to 100.”

I honestly thought he was joking.

I didn’t want to offend him, but I had to ask: “You’re telling me that someone spends several hours every day copying information from emails into another system? And more importantly: This was the reason we couldn’t play tennis?"

A few days later, I called him and said: “I haven’t stopped thinking about that process. If you want, I can take a closer look at the system and see whether there’s a smarter approach.”

There was.

It turned out that their order-management system had a well-documented API.
So I built a small agent that monitored the order inbox, extracted the relevant information and transferred it into the system automatically.

The complete build, testing and launch took around two days.
Afterwards, approximately 90% of the incoming orders could be processed automatically.
Only the unusual cases still needed human attention.

Two days of building removed roughly two to three hours of boring manual work from every working day.

And that was the moment I realized: Sometimes smaller companies just need someone to notice the stupid process, understand it and build the simple missing piece.

In other words: That cancelled tennis match became the beginning of Hagbard Studio.
