A company can approve a plan to publish fifty articles without anyone having the authority to say the thirty-first article should not exist.

The plan has a budget. The topics have owners. Writers have deadlines. Someone has already put the titles in a spreadsheet, which gives them the faint but powerful appearance of inevitability.

Then the research begins. One proposed article repeats a page the company already has. Another answers a question the product team says customers never ask. A third makes a claim that sounds plausible until someone requests evidence. Each concern is reasonable. None is quite large enough to stop the forward momentum of the calendar.

I have worked at agencies long enough to know why this happens. The agency promised a volume of work. The client approved it. The people closest to the subject may be brought in to review a draft, but rarely to decide whether the draft is worth writing. At that point, saying no creates a new task: someone has to replace the article, revise the plan, and explain the change.

I have worked in house long enough to know why this happens too. Especially start ups. Pick a tactic you know has proven itself more than once and exploit the hell out of it and don't ever slow down even if your increased pace is exactly what's hurting you.

Publishing it - whatever it is - just becomes easier. The article gets an approval, a URL, and perhaps six impressions from a query nobody knew they were targeting.

AI makes that decision easier to avoid. Production can move faster than the people who know the subject can review it. A team can fill a calendar before it has answered whether the business has anything useful to add.

I would like more content plans to include permission to stop. 

If the research turns up nothing distinctive, the team should be able to use that time to improve an existing page, interview someone who knows the subject, or investigate a better question. 

The agency and client would have to agree in advance that changing the plan can be evidence of judgment rather than a failure to deliver it.

Otherwise, the calendar becomes the customer. It asked for fifty articles, and by God, it will get fifty articles.
