You did everything right.
You earned the STEM degree. Maybe even the PhD.

So why does “entry-level” suddenly require skills nobody taught you?
You are not underqualified.
You may have simply graduated into a profession that changed while you were training for it.

Think about the timing.
If you started your degree or PhD in 2020 or 2021, you made that decision before generative AI became an everyday work tool.
Before the EU AI Act.
Before Europe accelerated investment in batteries, semiconductors, clean energy and cybersecurity.
Before ongoing EU clinical trials moved fully under the Clinical Trials Regulation.
Five years later, the title of your profession may look familiar.

The actual job does not.
Software graduates are expected to evaluate AI-generated code.
Automotive engineers need battery, software and power-electronics knowledge.

Life-science PhDs are being pulled towards manufacturing, quality, regulatory and data-intensive roles—often without being shown how their research experience translates.

This is the five-year gap:
The distance between the profession you prepared for and the profession now hiring. It can feel deeply personal when applications go unanswered.
But the gap is not proof that you chose the wrong degree, failed to prepare or need to start again.

It is a timing effect—and it can be navigated.
I explored what changed across seven European STEM professions, why the bridge into the first role is breaking, and what graduates can do next.
