Last week, I attended Microsoft's Frontier Tour in London. At 2:30pm, the boss sent me three cryptic WhatsApp messages:

"Copilot Managed Runtime." "Interesting." "Have a read."

I'd never heard the term. Hours later, I realised Microsoft might have quietly solved a big problem with AI-generated software: Building the app is easy now. Somebody still has to run it.

As a former IT and Infrastructure Manager, I saw software deployed. Some brilliant. Some disastrous. Every application raised the same questions. Where does the data live? How is it secured? What infrastructure does it need? How does it scale? Who patches it?
Those questions haven't disappeared because AI can write software.

GitHub Copilot, Claude, Codex and others have reduced the expertise needed to turn an idea into working software. It's now trivially easy to build crappy apps and deploy them to unmanaged cloud services.

But then Copilot Code came along.

Yesterday, a colleague showed me the spreadsheets her role depends on. Some are slow and inconsistent. Some are effectively mission-critical. One is the Company Car Tracker.
Lose it and she'd be reconstructing vehicle, financial and lease information from emails, documents, Teams messages and phone calls. Hell on Earth.

So I opened Copilot Code, told it I was temporarily our Fleet Manager and described the app I wanted. 20 minutes and 1,100 AI Credits later, around £11, I had a browser-based fleet tracking app.

But that's not the interesting bit. AI can already build CRUD apps. The interesting bit is that I didn't have to become its infrastructure team afterwards. Normally, I'd still need to decide on the database, hosting, authentication, deployment and security. Without my development background, I might not even know those were questions I needed to ask.

With Copilot Code, I didn't provision a database, create an app registration, choose a hosting platform or build a deployment pipeline. Copilot Managed Runtime took care of much of that plumbing.

There's an irony here.

Generative AI could create an explosion of shadow IT. When anybody can build an app in an afternoon, anybody can build one that IT doesn't know exists.

Copilot Managed Runtime tries to square that circle: make app development more accessible without making it invisible to IT. Maybe the answer to shadow IT isn't stopping people from building things. Maybe it's giving them somewhere governed to build them.

I think Microsoft missed the mark with Cowork. But with Copilot Managed Runtime and Copilot Code, I think they've hit the nail on the head. I wouldn't give this to every employee.
But in the right hands, someone maintaining a mission-critical spreadsheet doesn't need to become a developer, and their helper doesn't need to become a DBA, infrastructure engineer and identity architect.

If these tools deliver, we might finally end the reign of spreadsheets pretending to be databases.

Sounds like heaven to me.
