What happens between a website enquiry and a booked appointment?

Someone searches for a psychiatrist, finds our website, shares a little about what they’re struggling with, and leaves their contact details.
And yet, not everyone who enquires goes on to book an appointment.
Where did we lose this person?

As someone building a psychiatric practice, this is something I’ve been thinking about. Our website currently allows people to submit appointment enquiries, but bookings are still handled manually. It gives us control over scheduling, but it also means there’s a gap between someone reaching out and us getting back to them.
And I wonder what happens in that gap.

1. We replied too late.
Someone fills out the form at night, perhaps after a particularly difficult day. By the time we get through walk-ins, WhatsApp enquiries and everything else the next day, it’s already afternoon. They’re at work, they don’t have the privacy to speak, or the moment in which they felt ready to reach out has passed.

We have an automated confirmation message, but is that enough? Should we tell people when they can expect a response? Make our contact details more accessible?

2. We didn't match their expectations.
Sometimes, the barrier is financial. Someone goes through our profile, explains their concerns, and then discovers that our fees don't fit their budget.
We offer subsidised slots and pro bono sessions, but these have their limits. And I don't want someone who cannot access our services to leave with nothing useful.

Could we create evidence-based mental health resources on our website? Not generic wellness tips or a replacement for therapy, but genuinely useful information that people can access regardless of whether they become our patients.

3. There are probably other barriers, too.
Uncertainty about what to expect, difficulty finding a suitable appointment, or simply not being ready to take the next step.

Of course, not every enquiry that doesn't convert is a failure on our part. People have their own reasons for not proceeding, and we cannot solve all of them.
But I do think it's worth examining the process rather than accepting that some enquiries will inevitably go nowhere.
My next step is to start implementing some of these ideas and see what changes.
One thing I’m learning while building a psychiatric practice is that the work doesn't begin when someone walks into the consultation room. There is an entire patient journey before that, and we need to pay attention to it, too.

I started writing this to understand why people don't book after enquiring. I haven't found all the answers, but I now have a few things I want to try.

Sometimes, writing about a problem is also how you begin to work through it.
