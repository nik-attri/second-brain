Response Evaluation: When AI Tells You Something, You Still Have to Decide
Last week I posted about the Anthropic/Claude framework and the three pillars that stuck with me: prompt engineering, response evaluation, and context.

The first one (prompt engineering) got great traction. People get it: ask specific questions, get specific answers. That’s craft.

But response evaluation is harder. It's the one nobody wants to talk about. Because it requires saying "no" to the AI.

The Real Problem with AI in Governance
Here’s what nobody tells you in the hype cycle: the tool isn't the bottleneck. Judgment is.

When I ask Claude a governance question, I get an answer in seconds. But I can't just blindly implement it. I have to ask:
Does this pass our internal standards?
Does this respect vendor relationships?
Is this compliant with our regulatory framework?
Would this create risk we haven't mitigated?

That's not prompt engineering. That's building guardrails. And guardrails are where governance lives.

What Response Evaluation Actually Looks Like

In multi-country delivery, you are constantly making judgment calls. Your director asks: "Should we escalate this vendor issue?"

Claude can help you integrate and organize the data. But you make the call.

Claude can extract signal. It can pattern-match. It can consolidate information faster than you ever could manually. But it doesn't know your risk tolerance. It doesn't understand your vendor relationship nuances. It doesn't carry the weight of decisions that affect real people.

Most people skip the evaluation step. They see a polished answer and assume it's right. That's when things break.

Three Guardrails That Matter

Compliance First: Claude suggested streamlining an approval process for one of our projects to speed up delivery velocity. Makes sense on paper. But in one specific market, that streamlined process violated employment notification requirements. In another, it broke vendor protocols. Claude didn't know that. I had to.

Relationship Reality: Claude sees data patterns; it doesn't see the conversation you had with a critical vendor three weeks ago. It doesn't know if a vendor is irreplaceable due to market constraints.

Human Stakes: Claude optimizes for efficiency. Leaders must optimize for people. That's not anti-AI. It's pro-governance.

How This Changes Your Workflow
Don't use Claude as a decision layer. Use it as a signal-extraction layer.
Next time, ask it:
"What patterns do you see in this data?"
"What are the trade-offs I should consider?"
"What blind spots am I missing here?"
Then, you do the heavy lifting. You weigh the relationships. You carry the human stakes forward.
That's responsible governance in the age of AI.
