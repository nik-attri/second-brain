The median US company spends $12 per employee per month on AI. The top 1% spend $7,400. Neither number appears in any salary benchmark I have ever used.

Andrey Doronichev cited Ramp's data in his closing keynote at Tech Race Summit, and I went back to check it afterwards. Ramp tracks card and invoice spend across 70,000 American businesses. In July the median firm paid $11.95 per employee per month on AI, roughly one seat on a chat subscription. The top 10% paid $650. The top 1% paid $7,400, per person, per month.

Do the arithmetic for a team of ten engineers at the top end and you get $74,000 a month on tokens, next to a payroll that in most of Europe would be smaller than that. Doronichev's reading was that this only makes sense if what companies are buying is a workforce, and I think he is right.

Here is what this does to my job.

For sixteen years the cost of an engineer was one number: the salary, plus whatever the market added in equity and benefits. I could benchmark it by city, level, and stack in two hours. Now there is a second line, and it is invisible to every benchmark I use. Two engineers on the same salary can cost a company amounts that differ by hundreds of dollars a month, depending on how many agents they run. Nobody in a hiring brief has mentioned that to me yet.

And nobody has decided what that second line is. The same $650 a month can buy two different things. More output, so the company gets more product for the same headcount. Or the same output in fewer hours, with the saved time going back to the engineer's own life. Only one of those shows up in the company's numbers, and the conversations I had in the corridors in Warsaw split about evenly on which one is happening.

One more number from the same source, because it cuts against the panic. Ramp linked its spend data to workforce records across 21,000 firms and found that the heaviest AI spenders grew headcount by about 10% and entry-level hiring by 12%, while low spenders showed no change. Correlation, as the authors say, and those firms were already growing. But the companies paying the largest token bills are not, so far, the ones cutting people.

So there is a question I now want to ask every client at intake, and I have never asked it before: what is the tooling budget per engineer, and is it inside the band or on top of it?
Has anyone here been asked in an interview what their token budget would be?
