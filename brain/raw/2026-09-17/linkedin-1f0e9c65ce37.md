The Future of Laboratory Digitalisation
One question. One strategic insight. One practical action.

Today’s question
Why do science and research organisations choose Laboperator?
Jeroen de Haas, our Chief Product Officer, considers what drives the decision.

The current reality
Most laboratories already have sophisticated technology. They have LIMS and ELNs, automated equipment, analytical instruments and highly skilled scientists. Yet these capabilities do not necessarily operate as one system.
Scientists still move between applications, coordinate activities manually, enter information more than once and track the progress of workflows across different tools. The result is often slower throughput, inconsistent execution and greater reliance on individual knowledge.

The constraint, in other words, is not necessarily the technology itself. It is the way that technology is connected and coordinated at the point where science is actually performed.

What most laboratories get wrong
The usual response is to look for another solution to address the next problem. But a collection of point solutions does not automatically become an integrated laboratory.

A robot can automate a task. A #LIMS can manage samples. An #ELN can document an experiment. The more difficult question is how these systems, instruments and people work together throughout the process. That is the gap customers increasingly recognise.

One strategic insight
According to Grand View Research, Inc, the global life science tools market was valued at $184.3bn in 2025 and is forecast to reach $401.0bn by 2033, at a CAGR of 10.2%. As laboratories continue to invest in new instruments, automation and digital systems, the value of those investments will depend increasingly on how well they work together.

The strategic opportunity is therefore not simply to add more technology. It is to create an operational layer that connects existing technology and allows the laboratory to function as a coordinated whole. This is where Laboperator is different. Rather than replacing existing systems or becoming another system of record, it provides an execution layer that connects instruments, systems, workflows and the people working at the bench.

One practical action
Start with the work rather than the technology. Take one important laboratory process and follow it from beginning to end. Identify where scientists have to change systems, where information is entered manually, where activities have to be coordinated by handand where progress depends on individual knowledge.

Those points often reveal where better orchestration can have a greater impact than another isolated technology investment.
