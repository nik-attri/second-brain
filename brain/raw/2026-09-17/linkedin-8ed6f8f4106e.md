When did verifying cybersecurity controls become a “money grab”?

I keep seeing this sentiment in the Defense Industrial Base, and quite frankly, I’m tired of it.

We’re talking about organizations entrusted with information that can have national security implications. Systems supporting the people who build, supply, equip, and sustain the warfighter. And controls that are supposed to protect that information.

So when did asking someone to verify that those controls actually exist, operate, and work as intended become the problem?

Who assumes the risk when they don’t?

The contractor? The prime? The Department? The taxpayer? The warfighter?

Risk doesn’t stop at a company’s front door. It moves through the supply chain.

DFARS 252.204-7012 requires contractors to provide adequate security for covered contractor information systems. CMMC exists because “we have the controls” has to mean more than “we wrote them down.” 

And why question the motivation of those who are trying to fix a critical issue?  People who have spent hundreds of hours learning requirements, participating in working groups, mentoring, training, building assessment programs, and trying to make this ecosystem better.

People who did it because they believe the mission matters.

Because the warfighter matters.

Because the taxpayer deserves better than security ‘theater’.

And somehow the reward is:

“Money grab.”

Really?

By all means, challenge the cost, methods, and bureaucracy. Challenge everything.

That’s how good programs get better.

But don’t confuse making assurance better with ELIMINATING assurance.

I support reducing barriers. I support helping small businesses. I support eliminating duplication and requirements that don’t improve security.

But there is a line we cannot cross.

That line is where making compliance easier becomes making it easier to claim compliance without meaningful assurance that the underlying security actually exists.

Trust me when I say, our adversaries don’t give a crap about what the assessment costs.

They care whether they can get in.

And if they do, nobody is going to say:

“Well, at least we saved money on the assessment.”

We’re going to ask what was accessed, what was stolen, how far it spread, and what the consequences are.

That’s the bill nobody puts on the spreadsheet.

So before we call cybersecurity assessments a “money grab,” maybe we should ask a different question:

What is the price of being wrong?

I don’t want that risk landing on the warfighter.

And we shouldn’t have to pay it as a taxpayer.

And I sure as hell don’t want us discovering after a breach that the control we thought was protecting our data never actually worked.

Verify it.

Fix what doesn’t work.

Then verify it again.

That’s not a money grab.

That’s ACCOUNTABILITY.

And when the information we’re protecting can affect national security, accountability isn’t a luxury.

It’s part of the job.
