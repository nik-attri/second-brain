My highest GCSE grade was an 8 in Maths, but I still chose to do all humanities at A level. Here's why.

At the start of year 11, I was moved from second set maths to top set which at first didn't feel like much of a jump. But little did I know, at my school, you couldn't make a bigger one. It was the difference between having to explain basic concepts to everyone all lesson, to being surrounded by a bunch of mini-Einsteins who spent their free time learning how to manually square root. Naturally I didn't quite match this bunch, but that's exactly why I needed to be there.

Now, over the year I made two very important realisations:

1) I didn't actually like being there. 
Every single moment of that class was comprised of the worry I was just re-using the same skills over and over again and not going anywhere. It stressed me out, to the point where I would do three past papers in one sitting and still felt I wasn't where I needed to be. And it wasn't just me. As I occasionally looked around the room every so often, almost every other person was in the same state. Veins on their forehead bulging, eyes on the verge of tears, and pages and pages of work for a question shorter than my pinky. Thankfully, the work paid off and no-one got lower than a 7, but at what cost? We put ourselves through that, to get shown a number that tells us we're allowed to put ourselves through it again? That's not living, that's just surviving. I would much rather sit in a history class and nerd out, just to get an average grade, than go through that again. 

2) I didn't feel valued being there. 
Whenever we would do a set of practise questions, I'd work so hard to make sure I had every single one right. Then sir would put the answers up and it would be identical. Identical. Not similar, not a different method, Identical. Sure, it meant I was right but was it really ME being right? I felt like a unit. Programmed, mundane, replaceable. No one actually cared about what I did, because there were rooms full of people that could do it just as well. It was never like that in English. Sure, everyone has similar skills,, but nobody else wrote MY essays using MY interpretations of the text. I was actually producing my own work rather than just being a carbon copy of the guy next to me.

Now do I believe no-one should go into STEM? Absolutely not. Stem workers are essential to the functions of everyday life, and we would probably fall apart without them. All I'm trying to say is that people need to understand the difference between these fields, and why sitting in a room discussing right and wrong is just as noble as discussing the purpose of the mitochondria.
