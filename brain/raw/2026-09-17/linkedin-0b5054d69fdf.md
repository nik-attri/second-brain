Are you letting a well‑meaning advisor turn your résumé into a puffed‑up brochure instead of a truthful showcase?

I see this pattern far too often: a professional-sometimes a career coach, a vocational counselor, or a recruiter-offers enthusiastic encouragement, rewrites your résumé with extra jargon, and pushes tactics like “apply in person” because they once heard it worked. The advice feels supportive, but it can mask a deeper mismatch between your actual experience and the expectations of hiring managers. When the document is inflated, you lose credibility the moment an interview probes the gaps, and you waste precious time chasing roles that aren’t a realistic fit.

The remedy is simple but requires discipline. First, treat any external input as a hypothesis, not a mandate. Compare every bullet point against a concrete example you can discuss confidently. If you can’t recount a specific achievement, the line belongs on a draft, not the final version. Strip the résumé down to what you truly did, quantifying impact only when you have evidence. A concise, one‑page résumé that tells a clear story beats a two‑page fluff piece every time.

Second, focus your job‑search energy on skill gaps rather than résumé length. Identify the core competencies required for the roles you want-be it data entry accuracy, calendar management, or customer service etiquette-and seek low‑cost ways to build them: short online courses, volunteer projects, or shadowing opportunities. When you can demonstrate a recent, relevant accomplishment, the conversation shifts from “what you claim” to “what you’ve proven.”

Finally, diversify your feedback loop. Instead of relying on a single counselor, run your résumé past a peer in the target field, a hiring manager you know, or a professional networking group. Their practical perspective will quickly expose any overstatements and suggest concrete edits. By anchoring your materials in reality and filling genuine skill gaps, you turn the job hunt from a gamble into a strategic progression.

When you own the narrative and back it with verifiable experience, you’ll walk into interviews with confidence, not anxiety.
