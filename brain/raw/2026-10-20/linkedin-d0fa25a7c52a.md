Why is Trump trying to stop Ukraine from striking Russian oil refineries?  The implicit assumption is that they inflict serious damage. But how much, and where?  

Štěpán Mikula and I have substantially revised our  study of the economic effects of the strikes: https://lnkd.in/ePvBK6eT. 

Compared with the previously circulated version, the new draft provides stronger causal evidence and traces the disruption through to Russia’s seaborne exports.

We combine a manually verified chronology of strikes with satellite detections night-time lights and thermal anomalies, weather data and cargo-level records of Russia’s seaborne petroleum-product exports. Our analysis covers 30 major Russian refineries. We follow each facility over time and compare it with refineries that had not yet been hit or were never hit.

After the first strike, night-time lights fall sharply and remains persistently lower. The decline is about 28% between 500m and 1 km from the refinery and 15% within 5 km. It weakens with distance and is no longer distinguishable from zero at 25 km. Using estimates from the literature linking night-time lights to GDP, these changes correspond approximately to a 5% decline in local economic activity within 5 km and around 10% in the band closest to the refinery.

The result is not simply due to machinery and lighting being switched off inside the plant. Using mapped refinery boundaries, we find that luminosity falls by 19% in the first kilometre outside the industrial perimeter and by 17% in the second.

To estimate the effect of an individual strike, we use night-time winds at the altitudes flown by long-range drones.  When winds from Ukraine towards a refinery are more favorable, the probability of a strike on that facility increases. This variation allows us to separate the response to a marginal strike induced by wind conditions from the strategic selection of targets.  Such a strike reduces luminosity in the surrounding area during the first 60 days. The estimate then returns close to zero.

The campaign as a whole has more persistent effects. Many refineries are hit repeatedly, often before the effects of earlier strikes have dissipated. Slow repairs and reluctance to restore exposed facilities to full capacity may also contribute.

We then examine whether the disruption reaches exports. Flows more closely linked to a struck refinery fall most clearly in the month of the strike. The contraction also reaches the foreign markets they normally served. But total Russian exports to those markets do not fall robustly: other terminals appear to redirect shipments between strikes.
