A week or so ago, there was a bit of a to-do on social media because the Conservatives had incorrectly cited the Climate Change Committee’s estimate of the cost of Net Zero at something like £900 billion rather than the actual figure of around £500 billion.

The precise numbers don’t matter, because both figures are completely ridiculous. Based on current costs, Net Zero will cost upwards of £6 trillion, and then only so long as you are willing to interpret “Net Zero” in a liberal fashion and not get too worried about a few residual tens of millions of tons of carbon dioxide being emitted. If you feel strongly about it, you can spend much, much more to eliminate the remainder, but the cost is mad enough already, so I recommend against it.

£6 trillion is about £1.8 trillion more than stopping where we are and about £2.4 trillion more than going back to fossil fuels as wind and solar farms retire. If you can find a way to close them down faster, there are even larger savings available.

In case you find this hard to believe, I am publishing the spreadsheet model that produced these numbers. At least then we can discuss the numbers in a rational way. 

It’s a simplified system: supply from renewables plus gas, and a store if you want it, current demand plus electrification of domestic heat and transport and some AI data centres. This means the model captures the big numbers (or at least those relating to the low-hanging fruit of decarbonisation), but is reasonably parsimonious, so you can understand how your decisions affect the result. You can play around with the assumptions to your heart’s content, or challenge my assumptions or whatever.

It would be nice to compare my model with the Climate Change Committee’s, so we can understand how they arrive at a figure of £500 billion. Unfortunately, they are determined that the general public will not ever know how they did it. Reasonable people will assume that they are reticent because they know their numbers are dodgy as hell.
