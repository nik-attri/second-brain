At 11:37 PM, I was sitting on the floor counting peanut butter jars.

Not because I was doing inventory.

Because something didn’t add up.

There were cartons everywhere.

Labels.

Caps.

Empty boxes.

Half-opened cartons.

One calculator that nobody could find ten minutes earlier.

And me, sitting on the floor thinking:

“Where the hell did those 24 jars go?”

We had checked the production quantity.

We checked the dispatch quantity.

We checked the stock sheet.

Everything looked right.

Which, somehow, made it worse.

Because if the numbers had been wrong, we could fix the numbers.

But the numbers were behaving perfectly.

Reality wasn’t.

So we started counting again.

Carton by carton.

Then shelf by shelf.

Then opened cartons.

Then the cartons we had already counted because apparently we had reached the stage where nobody trusted anybody—including ourselves.

Around midnight, we found them.

Twenty-four jars.

Sitting inside a carton that had been marked as something else.

Nobody had stolen anything.

Nobody had made some massive operational mistake.

It was just a small human error in a very ordinary evening.

But I remember sitting there afterwards and thinking about how strange building a physical business actually is.

On paper, a business can look beautifully organised.

Numbers.

Spreadsheets.

Invoices.

Stock reports.

Purchase orders.

Everything has a column.

Everything has a number.

Then you walk into the warehouse and discover that twenty-four jars have decided to live somewhere completely different.

And suddenly the spreadsheet feels very confident for something that has never actually picked up a carton.

That night wasn’t a disaster.

It wasn’t even a particularly important problem.

But it showed me something I hadn’t fully appreciated before.

The closer you get to the real product, the less room there is for pretending that everything is under control.

Because physical businesses don’t run on spreadsheets alone.

They run on people remembering things.

People checking things.

People asking stupid questions.

People admitting, “I don’t know.”

People going back and counting the damn jars one more time.

And sometimes, at 12:15 in the night, they run on a founder sitting on the floor because that’s where the answer happens to be.

I still like spreadsheets.

I just trust them a little less than I used to.
