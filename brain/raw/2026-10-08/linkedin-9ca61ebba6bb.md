𝗪𝗵𝘆 𝗪𝗲 𝗠𝗮𝗱𝗲 𝗗𝗮𝘁𝗮 𝗦𝗵𝗮𝗿𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗔𝗻𝗼𝗻𝘆𝗺𝗼𝘂𝘀 𝗯𝘆 𝗗𝗲𝗳𝗮𝘂𝗹𝘁: We don't tell anyone where your vessel has been. I think that's the reason the whole program works.

When we built the cloud service that lets our users share their depth history maps, we could have made it a badge. Look at this yacht, look where she went, look at what she contributed. That would have been good marketing for us and good marketing for them.

But talk to enough captains and owner's reps and you hear the same worry pretty quickly. People are genuinely concerned about being tracked. Where the boat is, where the boat was, who was aboard. And it doesn't matter how good the cause is, if contributing means announcing your track, most vessels are simply never going to opt in.

So it's anonymous by default. We handle the privacy and the anonymity on the operator's behalf. The International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) holds the permissions from member states and sorts out where this can and can't be done, all internally, and anywhere that's opted in, the data goes to the global community at no charge. Once a vessel is hooked into our system, there is nothing the crew has to think about. No extra step on the bridge, no change to what they're already doing. (And if a customer does want their name shown to the world, it's possible. It's just not the default and something we need to implement manually.)

According to Seabed 2030, only 28.7% of the global ocean has ever been mapped. Captain Cook's measurements are still official data on some charts, because they're still the best measurements anyone has. In coastal areas it's about 50%, and most of that is the main shipping channels, because the agencies producing those surveys are charged with enabling commerce. Outside the channel, why map it?

Which is exactly where yachts go. The nooks and crannies. The anchorages that are interesting precisely because nobody has been there with a survey vessel.

The second piece is the unglamorous one, metadata. Community contributions from echo sounders are valuable, but do you know exactly where your echo sounder is mounted? Or your GPS antenna? Without those details the data is worth a lot less. Our depth history maps look a lot like the output of a survey sonar, and they carry high quality metadata along with them.

And it isn't only us. The International SeaKeepers Society has vessels running little data loggers that plug into existing echosounders. Fugro has donated an enormous amount of commercial survey data. We will never get there if nobody participates, but if everybody participates we can start making progress.

That's the why not. You're going there anyway. It costs you nothing and takes no time, and nobody finds out where you were.
