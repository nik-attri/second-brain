If AI does the junior work, where will seniors come from?

Junior employees have traditionally entered companies through a sacred ritual: receiving all the tasks nobody else wanted. Fix the tiny bug. Update the documentation. Prepare the spreadsheet. Move the button three pixels left, then move it back after Product sees it. It was not glamorous, but it was how people learned 🐣

Now AI can write boilerplate, summarize meetings, analyze data and generate tests before a junior has finished asking where the documentation is. Naturally, someone eventually asks: if a senior with AI can do the work faster, why hire juniors at all? Somewhere, an Excel spreadsheet achieves consciousness and approves the budget.

The problem is that junior work was never only about completing small tasks. It was training. Seniors became seniors by solving simple problems, making survivable mistakes and occasionally discovering exciting new ways to break production. You cannot replace that experience with a prompt saying, 'Explain software architecture like I’m five.' Removing junior tasks while expecting future seniors to appear is like deleting the tutorial level from a game and wondering why nobody can defeat the final boss. Yes, collecting mushrooms was repetitive. It was also where you learned which button swings the sword.

The evidence is not yet 'AI stole everyone’s job and bought a yacht.' Stanford University researchers found that AI’s overall employment impact remains small, although the difficult market for recent graduates may already be partly connected. Meanwhile, the World Economic Forum says more than one-third of young workers have jobs exposed to significant AI-driven change. Companies have not fired the interns, but they may be redesigning the internship 🤖

This does not mean juniors should spend three years manually writing boilerplate for character development. AI should remove repetitive work. But companies must replace that lost practice with better training. Let AI produce the first draft, then ask juniors to verify it. Let it generate tests, then ask what it missed. Give beginners smaller responsibilities, feedback and progressively harder problems.

Otherwise, companies will become more productive today by borrowing against their future expertise. Eventually, the current seniors will leave, retire or escape to a cabin where Jira cannot find them. The company will then publish a vacancy requiring ten years of experience with a framework released last Tuesday and complain about the talent shortage 🫠

AI can generate code, reports and presentations. What it cannot generate on demand is five years of judgement. For that, we still need juniors.
