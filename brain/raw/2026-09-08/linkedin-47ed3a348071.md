Last week a candidate wrote to tell me he had just signed with another company. Then he asked whether I still wanted to talk.
 
I said yes. Not to keep him warm for a future search. Because the conversation is the job, and right now the advice a technology leader needs has changed faster than most of them realise.
 
Three things are moving at once.
 
Sector expertise is worth more than it was two years ago. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on more than 1,000 employers covering 14 million workers, describes the fastest-growing roles as those combining technical skills with domain knowledge that cannot easily be automated. The generic CTO is increasingly commoditised. The CTO who understands payments, or logistics, or clinical data is not.
 
Building is back in the job description. SignalFire's 2026 State of Tech Talent report shows overall hiring at large tech companies down 25 percent since 2019, but engineering hiring down only 11 percent, and entry-level hiring down 65 percent. Teams are smaller, flatter and more senior. The companies and funds I work with now expect the person running technology to be able to sit down and ship, not only to manage those who do.
 
And the shape of the role itself is changing. In the mandates I see, a growing share of technology leadership needs are being met by interim, part-time or fractional arrangements rather than a full-time hire, especially in portfolio companies between two stages of growth. These mandates are no longer a fallback for people between jobs. For some leaders they are the better career, and the conversation about a next move now includes whether it should be a full-time one at all.
 
None of this appears in the offer a candidate has just signed. So for an hour, with nothing to sell, I tell him what I think of his package, how his new company is perceived by the funds I work with, where his domain will be in three years, and what he should keep building so that his next move is a choice rather than a reaction.
 
Most firms treat that hour as a cost. I treat it as the reason people call me back years later, and the reason they recommend me to people I have never met.
 
You do not build a talent pool by collecting names. You build it by being useful to people at the moment when nobody else has a reason to be.
 
Executive search is not a transaction. It is a series of conversations, and most of them happen when nobody needs anything.
