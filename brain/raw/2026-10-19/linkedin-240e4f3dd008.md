The global entertainment and media industry generates $3.5 trillion a year.
People watch more video, stream more music, and spend more hours on screens than at any point in human history.
So why does the industry feel like it is in the middle of a nervous breakdown?
After 14 years in senior technical and operational roles across TV, media, and on-demand, I kept coming back to that question while watching the plumbing of this business up close.
We talk endlessly about “content,” “platforms,” and “disruption.”
We talk far less about the three forces that have governed every era of media for the last 150 years:
Greed. Fear. Scarcity.

Today, my new book, Greed, Fear, Scarcity: The Birth and Death of Entertainment, is live on Amazon in Paperback and Kindle.
For a century, the entertainment business did not sell stories. It sold scarcity:
• Limited cinema seats and broadcast hours.
• Strict release windows and the cable bundle.
• A handful of gatekeepers who decided what lived and died.

Every time a new technology arrived, the same cycle repeated:
First, Fear - incumbents tried to block it or laugh it off (“Who the hell wants to hear actors talk?” / “The VCR is to the American film producer what the Boston Strangler is to the woman home alone” / “Is the Albanian Army going to take over the world?”).
Then, Greed billions flooded in to build the next empire, from the cable boom to the Streaming Gold Rush. 
And then came the morning after.

Now, the scarcity of distribution and production has been destroyed. Supply is infinite. Algorithms have replaced schedulers. The Great Unbundling has quietly become the Great Rebundling. And generative AI is turning production into a Studio of One.

Across 27 illustrated chapters, sourced industry data, and a ledger of mega-mergers from AOL–Time Warner and Disney–Fox to Paramount–Warner Bros. Discovery, the book unpacks:

1.	The Architecture of Control - How windows, ratings, and bundles turned human attention into the most valuable commodity on Earth.

2.	The Streaming Hangover - Why growth hit a wall, why advertising came roaring back, and why studios are caught in the Franchise Trap.

3.	The Human Cost - What “synergy,” consolidation, strikes, and the freelance crisis have meant for the crews, editors, and musicians whose names roll up the end credits.

4.	The Last Scarcity - When AI can generate anything for next to nothing, what holds value? Finite human attention, live shared moments, and trust.

If you work in TV, film, streaming, broadcast engineering, music, gaming, or media strategy, this book was written for you. 

It connects the boardroom spreadsheet to the control room and the studio floor - giving you a clear perspective on how we got here and where your part of the industry is heading next.
