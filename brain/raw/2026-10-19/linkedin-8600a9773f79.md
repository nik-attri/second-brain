Some missed screenings are missed before anyone picks up a camera.

A UC Irvine study in the Journal of the American College of Radiology (published online 12 Oct) looks at a problem that sounds boring and isn't: working out who is even eligible to be screened.

Lung cancer screening in the US depends on smoking history, and uptake is just 18.2% nationally. One barrier, the authors write, is that structured smoking fields in the health record are often incomplete, so automated eligibility checks miss people.

So they built a rule-based natural language processing pipeline that reads smoking history from free-text clinical notes. In a manually reviewed sample of 2,200 notes, reviewers confirmed 58 eligible patients. The NLP found 38 of them (66%). The structured fields found 12 (21%). Thirty were found only by reading the notes.

The line that stays with me: "Efforts relying solely on physicians to modify documentation and workflows are unlikely to substantially improve screening."

The authors are careful about the limits. One health system, a few notes per patient, and they did not measure whether anyone went on to complete a scan.

But the lesson travels. Malaysia misses about 4.8M diabetic eye screenings a year. It's tempting to treat that as only a test problem. Part of it is a finding-people problem, and the fix rarely starts with asking busy doctors to type more.

It's how we try to build at Selfie AI too. Fit the work that already happens in the clinic. Don't add a new chore.

Clinicians, where do patients who should be screened fall through the cracks in your clinic: before the test, during it, or after?
