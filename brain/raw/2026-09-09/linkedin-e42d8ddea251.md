“We could build that”
has cost more founders more money
than almost any sentence in tech.

When we started building 4admin, the hardest thing to resist was how much could be built. 
AI was moving fast, new capabilities were showing up constantly, and as an engineer, it's genuinely thrilling. 
You see some clever new technology, and your brain immediately starts sketching out how it could go into the product. 
Classic shiny object problem, and I had it badly.

Looking back, the biggest way I've grown as a founder was by getting better at deciding what not to build.

It matters more as we get bigger. 
There are AI companies in our space trying to automate huge chunks of the financial advice journey all at once, moving across meeting recording, admin, report writing, suitability reports, the lot. 
So naturally customers ask whether we're going to do the same. 
And technically, we could.

But every time you move into another part of the process, you're taking on another entire workflow to understand in real depth. 
You open Pandora's box, and once it's open, you can't half close it. 
We've deliberately stayed laser-focused on one problem and gone as deep as we possibly can on it. 
We’d much rather solve one thing exceptionally well than claim to do everything while half solving problems along the way.

Working with more enterprise customers has only reinforced this. 
Two firms doing what looks like the exact same job will operate completely differently underneath, so a solution built perfectly around one can be useless for the next. 
The complexity inside a single problem is enormous, and it's easy to underestimate until you're in it.

The lesson I'd pass on to earlier-stage founders is that progress sometimes means having the discipline to leave a genuinely good idea alone.
