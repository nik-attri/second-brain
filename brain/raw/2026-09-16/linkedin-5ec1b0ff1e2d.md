One of the most useful questions I have learned to ask in engineering is:

“Why does this process need to take this long?”

In semiconductor test development, developing one test program (TP) could typically take around 2 weeks.

One TP.

Now imagine a migration or NPI project requiring tens — or even hundreds — of test programs.

At that scale, 2 weeks per TP is no longer just a cycle-time problem. It becomes a scalability and engineering-capacity problem.

More engineers.
More parallel activities.
More coordination.
More waiting.
And potentially months of execution.

So we challenged the way the work was being done.

After redesigning the development approach, we reduced TP development from around 2 weeks to roughly 4 hours — and in some cases, even less.

The breakthrough did not come from asking engineers to work harder. It came from questioning the system.

Where were the repetitive steps?
Where were we manually recreating information?
Where were handoffs creating delays?
What could be standardized?
What could software do without requiring engineering judgment?

And most importantly: What really needed an engineer — and what didn't?

Once those questions were addressed, the development model changed significantly. This reinforced an important leadership lesson for me:

Engineering capacity is not only determined by headcount. It is determined by how effectively the engineering system allows people to use their expertise.

When you look at only one TP, saving almost 2 weeks is already significant. But when a project requires 50, 100 or potentially hundreds of TPs, the impact compounds.

It can mean:
→ significantly higher TP development throughput
→ less dependency on additional engineering resources
→ engineers spending more time on real technical problems
→ faster NPI and migration execution
→ better scalability across larger product portfolios
→ faster response to business demand

For me, that is the bigger story.

Not simply: “How do we develop one TP faster?”

But: “How do we build an engineering system capable of scaling from a few TPs to hundreds?”

And that raises the next question. If TP development is no longer the main bottleneck, what should we attack next?

Checkout?
Correlation?
Characterization?
Qualification?
Data analysis?

Or the way engineering knowledge itself is captured and reused? The goal should not simply be to make engineers faster.

The goal is to systematically remove work that no longer needs to consume engineering time.
