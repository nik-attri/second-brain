Align before accelerating. Simplify before automating.

Last week I wrote about organisational momentum — and why alignment matters.

There’s another part to that thought.

Step 7.

You know Step 7. It’s the thing we do because Step 6 happened.

It involves three systems, two hand-offs, an approval and probably a spreadsheet.

Nobody quite remembers why it exists.

But it does.

And we’ve become exceptionally good at doing it.

Now AI arrives.

We can potentially do Step 7 faster, cheaper and with less human intervention.

There’s just one awkward question.

Why the hell are we doing Step 7?

If simplification is so obvious, why does it remain so difficult in large organisations?

I think part of the answer is that complexity is often locally rational.

A customer problem happens. We add a check.

A risk materialises. We add an approval.

A regulator asks a question. We create evidence.

One system can’t talk to another. We create a hand-off.

Something goes wrong once. We create a process to make sure it never happens again.

Most were perfectly sensible responses to real problems at the time.

But organisations move on.

Technology changes. Customers change. Teams change. Policies accumulate. Controls overlap. Ownership fragments.

The original problem may even disappear.

Its solution often doesn’t.

Eventually everyone owns a perfectly defensible part of the process — but nobody quite owns the whole thing.

That’s why I believe challenging complexity is a leadership imperative. But to earn the right to simplify it, you first need to understand it.

Go to the work.

Zoom in before zooming out.

Talk to the people doing it. Follow the journey. Understand why the policy exists. See where customers struggle and where colleagues compensate for broken processes.

Sometimes you discover it doesn’t need another transformation programme. It just needs a tweak.

We need both leaps & tweaks.

I’ve seen this first-hand.

In one customer journey my team and I worked on, we improved customer experience by around 60 points before the major technology transformation. A lot came through what I call the 3Ps — People, Process and Policy.

Then came the technology leap — moving the experience forward significantly again.

The leap matters.

But so does knowing what needs a leap, what needs a tweak — and what shouldn’t exist at all.

Which brings me back to AI.

Our ability to automate is accelerating dramatically.

But there’s a risk our ability to question the work doesn’t accelerate with it.

We could end up with sophisticated technology sitting on top of accumulated complexity.

Doing things faster isn’t transformation if those things no longer need doing.

So perhaps the sequence matters:

Align before accelerating.

Simplify before automating.

Then use technology to amplify what remains.

Because before we build a much faster Step 7…

we should probably make sure we still need Step 7.
