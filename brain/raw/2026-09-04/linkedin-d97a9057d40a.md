Most "automate this workflow" conversations start in the wrong place: the tool.

Somebody wants a Slack bot, or a Zapier chain, or an agent that "handles email." The tool gets picked before anyone has written down what decision the automation is actually supposed to make, and what happens when it makes the wrong one.

We've started asking a different first question when a business comes to us: what is the cost of this thing being wrong once a week? Not "does this save time" — everything saves time on the happy path. The real design question is what the failure mode looks like, because that's what determines whether you need a full agent with judgment and guardrails, or just a script that runs on a schedule.

That distinction is the entire job, honestly. Anyone can wire an API to a language model. The engineering is deciding how much autonomy a given task deserves — where a system should act on its own, where it should draft and wait for a human, and where it shouldn't touch the process at all.

We don't just consult on this from a whiteboard. We run our own agent fleet in production — it trades with a public, graded track record, it watches its own infrastructure, and it ships the software we sell. That means we've felt what it costs when we get the autonomy boundary wrong on our own systems before we ever recommend a boundary to someone else's. Right now that fleet's lifetime trading PnL sits at a small loss — not a number we're hiding, because "small loss, fully instrumented, still running" is a more honest credential than a highlight reel.

If your team is trying to figure out what to automate first — or whether an "AI integration" someone's pitching you is actually a judgment system or just a fancy if-statement — that's the conversation we like having.
