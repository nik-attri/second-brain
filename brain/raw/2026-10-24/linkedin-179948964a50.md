“If you stopped sending half your reports tomorrow, how many people would actually notice?”

An interesting question, isn't it?

Daily reports. Weekly dashboards. Monthly MIS. Leadership scorecards. Operational reviews.

Some automated. Some manually prepared. Some requiring hours of data collection, validation and formatting.

And they continue to be produced, week after week, month after month.

But who is actually using them, and for what?

Imagine applying a Design Thinking lens to this everyday business practice.

Map every report and dashboard being produced.

Who requested it? Who prepares it? How much effort goes into it? Who receives it? How frequently is it shared?

And most importantly,  speak to the recipients.

When did you last use this report?
What decisions does it help you make?
What information do you actually need?
What would happen if you stopped receiving it?

One response might sound familiar:

“We've always been sending this report.”

Some reports are critical. Others may have outlived their purpose. Some contain useful information, but not necessarily in a form that enables decisions.

And that's where the problem begins to look different.

From “How might we make reporting more efficient?”

To “How might we ensure the information we produce helps people make better decisions?”

That opens up possibilities.

Consolidating reports. Eliminating duplication. Rethinking frequency. Redesigning dashboards around decisions rather than simply displaying available data.

The objective isn't fewer reports. It's more meaningful information and less effort spent producing what nobody needs.

That's what I value about Design Thinking.

It helps us question established practices, understand the people we serve and rethink how value is created.

Before automating another report, perhaps we should first ask whether it needs to exist.
