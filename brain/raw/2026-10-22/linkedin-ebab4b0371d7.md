It’s been nearly a month since the last edition of Zubaan. This is a bi-weekly newsletter, and I know I’ve kept you waiting.

Over the past few weeks, I’ve been following the protests led by Gen Z women in Delhi after the reported gang rape near Lady Shri Ram College, alongside the sexual assault allegations emerging from Cornell. I’ve been struck by the anger on the streets of Delhi, the humour and sharpness of the slogans, and the refusal of young women to accept restrictions on their freedom as the price of staying safe.

I’ve also noticed how little conversation about these protests has reached my LinkedIn feed. For a generation encouraged to use this platform to build professional networks, find opportunities and shape their careers, these protests speak to something fundamental about the futures young women are trying to build. Economic opportunity means little if the freedom to study, work and move through a city safely cannot be taken for granted.

That is where this Zubaan #18 begins. I write about why women’s economic independence cannot be separated from the conditions in which they live. I look at the barriers women face in the workplace, the jobs available to them, and the decisions that shape who gets to advance.

A few stories stood out.
👨‍👧 The “girl dad” effect. Research reported by The Economist finds that when male managers in small Danish firms had their first daughter, women’s earnings relative to men’s and their share of employment increased. The effect was linked to hiring and promotions. It raises an uncomfortable question about why women’s capabilities can depend on who is making the decisions.

📊 The leadership gap. The World Economic Forum’s Global Gender Gap Report 2026, drawing on LinkedIn Economic Graph data, estimates that closing the global gender gap in economic participation and opportunity could take 129 years. Across 57 economies, women account for 46% of entry-level professionals on LinkedIn, but just 23% of C-suite professionals.

🇮🇳 Why do so few women work in India? Shishir Gupta and Aalhya Sabharwal  examine the gap between rising female labour force participation and the availability of secure, productive employment. More women counted as working does not automatically mean more women have access to decent jobs.

⏳ 350 years to close the gap? Haishan Fu and Robin Mearns of The World Bank Group examine how slowly women’s labour force participation is catching up with men’s, alongside the barriers that keep women concentrated in insecure work and leave. 

I’m curious how others are thinking about this. Have you been following the Delhi protests, and do you see them as part of the conversation about women’s work and economic opportunity?
