Some of your best leadership thinking will happen when you’re nowhere near your desk.

Not in another strategy meeting, while staring at the spreadsheet, or answering “one last email”… and definitely not while convincing yourself that being constantly available is the same thing as being valuable.

Business owners and executives are paid to think.
… to notice
… to connect dots
… to see what others don’t. 

But clarity gets pretty hard to find when your brain never gets a minute to shut up.

I’ve seen this over and over in leadership.

You’re wrestling with a people issue.
A decision.
A growth problem.
Something that just isn’t clicking.

So naturally… you think about it harder.

Book another meeting.
Ask another person.
Open another spreadsheet.
Research another option.

Because surely the answer will appear if you just keep poking at it long enough.

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes you need to get the hell away from it…

Walk.
Golf.
Sit by the water.
Take the afternoon off.

Go somewhere your title, inbox and to-do list don’t get to come with you.

Because distance does something useful.

It gives your brain room to connect what it couldn’t connect while you were forcing an answer.

And suddenly, the people problem looks different.

You realize the conversation you need to have.

You notice you’ve been solving the wrong problem.

You see the decision underneath the decision.

Or you come back and think:, 
How did I not see that before?

That’s not laziness.
That’s leadership.

And there’s another piece we don’t talk about enough.

We work so damn hard to get somewhere, then barely arrive before asking… 

What’s next?

The next client.
The next target.
The next promotion.
The next growth milestone.

At some point, stepping away isn’t only about coming back sharper.

It’s about remembering to actually experience the life and business you worked so hard to build.

So if you’re stuck this week, try this:

1. Name the problem.
    What are you actually trying to figure out?
2. Stop feeding it.
    No more meetings, Googling, opinions or staring at it for a while.
3. Leave.
    Physically if you can. Mentally if you can’t.
4. Do something unrelated.
    Preferably something where your brain gets bored enough to wander.
5. Come back with one question:
    What can I see now that I couldn’t see when I was inside the problem?

Successful leaders don’t spend every waking minute thinking about the business.

They learn when to think.
And when to stop thinking long enough for clarity to catch up.

Sometimes getting away from the work is the work.

What’s one problem you might need to stop working on for a little while?
