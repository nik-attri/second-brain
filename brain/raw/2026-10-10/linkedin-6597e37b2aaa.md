A Shoulder Injury Can Take Away Your Movement. Can Robotics Help You Get It Back?
Your shoulder lets you reach, lift, dress, work, and perform countless everyday movements.
But this remarkable mobility comes with a vulnerability: after a stroke, traumatic brain injury, or serious orthopedic injury such as a rotator cuff tear, shoulder function can deteriorate dramatically.
The consequences can include pain, muscle spasticity, joint subluxation, contractures, and loss of independence.
Recovery is not just about moving the shoulder again. It is about delivering the right movements, with the right level of control, repeatedly enough to support functional recovery.
And that presents a challenge for traditional rehabilitation.
Manually guiding an impaired shoulder through hundreds of movements can be physically demanding for therapists, while assessing resistance and movement quality by hand can make objective measurement difficult.
🤖 Could robotic rehabilitation help bridge this gap?
Here are five ways robotics can support a new approach to shoulder rehabilitation:
🔹 More repetitions: Robotic systems can help deliver high-volume, controlled movement practice.
🔹 Controlled movement: Force and position feedback can help monitor resistance and respond to changes during movement.
🔹 A different approach to shoulder mechanics: Stationary rehabilitation robots offer an alternative to wearable exoskeletons for guiding complex joint movements.
🔹 Measurable movement: Markerless motion analysis can assess range of motion (ROM) and compensatory trunk movements without attaching physical sensors to the patient.
🔹 Different training modes: Passive, active-assisted, and resistive modes can support different rehabilitation needs and stages of recovery.
At Hash-Tech, we are developing two complementary technologies:
👁️ PhysioEye — markerless movement analysis to quantify movement and range of motion.
🦾 ErgoBot — robotic-assisted rehabilitation for upper- and lower-limb joints.
Together, they represent an approach that connects measuring movement with supporting its rehabilitation.
Because when injury takes away movement, recovery deserves more than guesswork. It deserves precise assessment, controlled rehabilitation, and tools that help clinicians do more.
