Unpopular opinion: If your job description tells me which Excel formulas to use, I automatically assume your management style is a nightmare.

I saw a listing yesterday asking for an "analyst" with:
→ Step-by-step instructions on how to format cells

→ Mandatory use of specific nested lookups

→ Exact daily procedures for manual reporting

That isn’t a job description. That’s a cry for help from a team drowning in micromanagement.

Then comes the interview:
Instead of asking how I diagnosed a $200k attribution discrepancy or automated an 8-hour reporting backlog, they ask:

"Can you write an INDEX/MATCH off the top of your head in 30 seconds?"

It feels less like a professional assessment and more like an audition for a 15-second YouTube Shorts reel.

Here is the reality high-performing candidates know:
• Anyone can Google a syntax error in 10 seconds.

• Anyone can copy-paste a formula from a forum.

• Knowing a flashy keyboard shortcut does not mean you understand business drivers, data integrity, or root-cause analysis.

When you hire for rigid procedures, you don’t attract problem-solvers—you attract button-pushers who wait to be told what to do.

If you want impact, tell me the business problem you need solved. Give me the messy data, set the expectation, and let me figure out how to get there.

If you care more about how I populate Column D than whether the insight moves the business forward, you don't need a team member. You need a macro.
