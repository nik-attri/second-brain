THE EDUCATION SYSTEM HAS A FUNNY WAY OF PREPARING YOUNG PEOPLE FOR A FUTURE THEY MIGHT NEVER GET.

Right, let's have a chat.
We've got a system that spends years telling young people how important their future is. Work hard
Get your qualifications
Make good choices
Think about your career
All very nice. Stick it on a motivational poster. Maybe put a picture of a sunset behind it.
But here's the problem.
What happens when the young person sitting in front of you doesn't fit the mould?
Not everybody is going to thrive in a classroom. Not everybody is going to light up when presented with another worksheet.
Some are brilliant with their hands.
Some are creative.
Some need to move, build, fix, make, explore.
Some just need somebody to look beyond the behaviour, the attendance figures or the latest set of predicted grades and actually see them.
And yet we seem remarkably good at offering young people one particular version of success.
Then we wonder why some of them walk away from it.
We're now talking about almost 150,000 16–18-year-olds in England who are not in education, employment or training.
Let that sink in for a minute.
That's a hell of a lot of young people who are currently disconnected from the opportunities we're constantly telling them they need.
And before anybody starts pointing fingers at schools, teachers or young people, let's take a breath.
Most people working on the frontline are doing everything they can with what they've got.
But perhaps we need to start looking at the bigger picture.
We've become very good at measuring progress. Attendance.
Attainment.
Behaviour.
Predicted grades.
Targets.
Interventions.
We've got spreadsheets for everything. Probably a spreadsheet somewhere measuring how many spreadsheets we've completed.
But are we actually giving young people enough different ways to succeed?
Where are the practical pathways?
Where is the meaningful work experience?
Where are the opportunities to discover a talent they didn't know they had?
Where are the employers, mentors and organisations prepared to open a door?
And perhaps most importantly, where is the flexibility to recognise that not every young person is going to take the same road?
We keep telling children to aim high.
Brilliant.
But we also need to build more than one ladder.
Because here's what worries me.
A young person who disengages from mainstream education isn't necessarily a young person who has disengaged from learning.
Sometimes they've just stopped believing that the version of education being offered has anything for them.
And there's a pretty big difference.
We shouldn't be measuring a young person's potential by how comfortably they fit into the system. Perhaps we should be measuring the system by how well it recognises their potential.
Now there's a conversation worth having.
What do you think? Are we genuinely creating enough alternative pathways for young people or are we still trying to squeeze everybody through the same doorway?
Let's have a word.
