Some of you will know that I like to make music.

In my 20s and 30s I spent my evenings writing music and my weekends lugging my guitar around London to set up for an hour and play for 40–60 minutes, either on my own, alongside a more talented and willing friend, or with the other six members of my band.
I always tried to take a recording from the venue's mixing desk and squirreled these away for an as yet undetermined purpose. Alas, I was always destined to be a three-chord bedroom rock star...

Then recently I discovered Suno.

For the uninitiated, Suno is a generative AI platform for creating music. But what interested me wasn't asking AI to write a song for me. It was seeing what it could do with songs i'd already written, performed and recorded. So I dug out some of those old recordings.

Songs where I wrote the lyrics. I wrote the chords. I came up with the melody. I played the guitar and sang the vocal, not always perfectly. And I started using AI to reinterpret, remaster and polish them into something much closer to what I'd always heard in my head.

And that raised an interesting question.
Are they still my songs?
I think they are.

AI didn't have the idea. It didn't live the experience that inspired the lyric. It didn't sit with a guitar trying different chords. It didn't play the original gig. But it has undoubtedly played a significant role in producing the finished result.

And that got me thinking about a debate we're increasingly having in my day job.
As organisations like Atos embrace AI, where should we draw the line between using AI to accelerate our work and outsourcing our thinking to AI?

If I use AI to turn my ideas, experience and original work into a better-produced song, is that fundamentally different from using AI to turn the expertise of our architects, consultants and engineers into better collateral, proposals, designs or client outcomes?
For me, the distinction is becoming clearer.
AI shouldn't be a substitute for expertise, originality or accountability. It should be an amplifier of them.

The value isn't in how many hours somebody spent manually creating the PowerPoint, writing the document or producing the code. The value is in understanding the problem, applying the right expertise and judgement, challenging the answer, and ultimately being accountable for the outcome. Perhaps we therefore need to stop asking:

"Did AI create this?"
And start asking:
"Where did the thinking come from, who validated it, and who stands behind the result?"

I'm still not entirely sure whether using AI makes me a musician with a new production tool, or just a three-chord bedroom rock star with significantly better technology.
But I'm having a lot of fun finding out!

And I suspect we're going to be asking versions of the same question in our professional lives for quite some time.
