Perhaps AI has an accounting problem before it has an ROI problem. Or both.

A recent example made me think about this: Creating my last presentation with AI cost $101.80.

Because that number is so precise, it immediately triggers a reasonable question:
Was it worth it?

But the comparison we make is rarely as precise.

If I had built the same presentation manually in PowerPoint, it would have taken several additional hours. Those hours also have a cost.

But that cost is bundled into payroll. It is not attributed to this specific presentation or task.

The AI cost is metered.
The human alternative is treated as the baseline.

This creates an asymmetric burden of proof: AI spending has to justify itself at task level, while the cost of the established way of working remains largely unexamined.

This is particularly relevant for personal AI enablement. Its impact can be broad, but it is difficult to attribute through traditional business KPIs.

I do not think the answer is tracking every employee’s time and activity. That would give us more data, but not necessarily better management.
AI cost transparency is valuable. But if only the new cost is transparent, it can also distort the decision.

Perhaps the most useful question is not whether AI should have cost $101.80.

Perhaps we should ask:
- Why does this presentation require so many hours or dollars in the first place?
- Is that level of quality actually necessary?
- Are our standards appropriately defined?
- Do we even need a presentation?

A fair comparison should not only question the new cost. It should also challenge the old way of working.
