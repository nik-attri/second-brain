A message can sound exactly right and still come from the wrong person.

Imagine starting the day with a polished request that appears to come from a colleague. It mentions a familiar project and asks you to sign in through a link to review an urgent document.

The writing feels natural. The context feels relevant. Neither confirms who controls the message or where the link leads.

For requests involving access, sensitive information or changes to an established process, I would verify through a route I already trust. That could mean opening the usual application directly or contacting the colleague through an existing company directory entry, rather than using contact details supplied in the message.

This matters when designing AI workflows too. An assistant may summarise an incoming request accurately without establishing that the sender is authorised to make it.

I would keep that distinction visible: “The message requests access” is a description of the content. Granting access requires separate identity and permission checks.

For a small team, a useful starting point is to agree which requests always need independent verification and where suspicious messages should be reported. Make that route easy to find before someone is under pressure.

The practical habit is simple: when a message asks you to cross a sensitive boundary, verify the request through a trusted channel before acting.
