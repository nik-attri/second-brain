Unfilled vacancies cost UK businesses £72.8bn last year. About £14,200 each, according to research Cebr did for the REC, published last week.
I expect to see that number in a lot of recruiter outreach this week. I don't think it'll do much there.
A national average is an argument for the industry. It isn't a reason for one hiring manager to pick up the phone. Nobody signs off spend because of a figure about everyone else.
The more useful part of the report is the mechanism behind the number:
The cost estimate assumes a vacancy stays open about seven weeks.
57.5% of businesses with unfilled vacancies reported at least one linked employee departure. One vacancy becomes two.
A vacancy costs a small business roughly 37 days of lost output, against about 22 for a large employer.
Each of those is something you can see from the outside, if you look at a specific company rather than a market:
An ad that's still live, or has been reposted, well past seven weeks.
A second role appearing in the same team as one that's already open.
A small firm carrying an open role it clearly can't absorb for long.
That's the conversation worth starting. Not "vacancies cost £14,200", but "your operations manager role has been up since September, and now there's a second one in the same team. How's that landing on the people covering it?"
It's their number, not the country's. And it shows you looked.
One caveat: an old ad isn't always a struggling hire. Some firms leave ads up to build a pipeline, and some reposts are just admin. It's a reason to look closer, not a reason to assume.
The REC research is worth reading in full. Just don't forward the headline to your clients. Find the vacancy it's describing.
