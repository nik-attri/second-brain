It's been 6 months since I started my founder journey, so it's time for a self-reflection. Guess what has cost me the most financially running a tech consulting business? (Answer at the end.)

If you asked me:
- Did you make a lot of revenue? Hell NO!
- Do you regret the move? Hell NO! (Well, a little. If I'd waited to be made redundant, a severance pack would have given me a longer runway.)
- Did you learn a lot, and do you love it? Hell YES and YES!!!

These 6 months felt like 6 years, and I've loved every bit of it: the things I've tried, the lessons I've learned, the people I've met and worked with, and how much my personality has changed. I only wish I'd started 10 years ago, before I had family responsibilities or financial pressure.

Some uncomfortable numbers I'm not ashamed to share:
- Total revenue: $7,816 (plus $600 still owed for a completed project)
- Clients served: 8
- Organic clients: 7 of 8 (87.5%)
- Projects completed: 9
- First client and first revenue: month 3
- Next committed project: $5,000, starting mid-December

For a service-based tech consulting business, that's really not much. A few FOMO moments:
- My 6-month revenue isn't even half of what I used to earn in a single month.
- A mate I met this week said: "I made $25k from one side project with a Kiwi company."
- Another mate I met a few weeks ago has delivered heaps of client projects and made crazy money. He's even helping BYD Australia run marketing campaigns.
- A founder friend asked: "How do you even make a living from this?" 🫠 

As of now, I can't. But who knows, maybe next year? 😆 I've tried so many random things that consulting only got about 60% of my capacity.

Things I tried that didn't work out well:
1. Posting the usual way across multiple social channels.
2. Several utility web apps to promote my consulting service.
3. Consulting small restaurants, barber shops and tradies across Australia.
4. 15+ door-knocks on local community businesses.
5. A small Mac utility app.
6. A cold-start e-commerce validation.
7. Google Ads for a client with low organic traffic, an old-fashioned WordPress site and lots of UGC social videos.
8. Cross-border trading validation: not only capital-intensive, but also time and regulation-intensive.
9. Co-founding another business.
10. Selling fluffy panda collectible toys.
11. Sourcing labels and packaging from China for a local sports drink business.
12. Sourcing AI wearable hardware from China for a founder friend.
13. Finding qualified enterprise leads for a founder friend.
14. Finding Australian buyers for a Chinese automotive OEM.
15. Landing deals through a Chinese agri-tech fair group.
16. Helping an Australian alpaca quilt maker localise in China.
17. Interviewing 10+ offshore VAs, then onboarding one VA and one social content creator for about a month.
18. Interviewing 5 uni students as interns. Both sides were happy to go ahead, but the uni said they expect more established companies with 5+ employees.
19. And plenty more I can't even remember.

Each of these took days or weeks to finish or conclude. Many were just rough trials, and with more time and effort, things might have gone differently.

Despite all that, there were plenty of happy moments:
1. Some unconventional social posting and marketing tricks actually work.
2. $0 ad spend.
3. Clients came from multiple channels, with no prior relationship with me.
4. I deliberately capped my inbound traffic so I had time to serve existing clients while still trying other stuff.
5. One client found me via Google Search, and another via ChatGPT. I couldn't believe it when I heard.
6. One client referred two more, and I simply replicated the same system for both.
7. Two clients booked consulting sessions straight from my calendar. At first I thought they were regular meetings I'd forgotten about.
8. I have 10+ customer acquisition channels I know will work, but haven't had time to try yet.
9. Next month's committed project is an IoT control and payment system for a local billiards hall. It moves me to a higher price tag (still way cheaper than other providers, lol), but it's progress.
10. A more challenging and fun hardware idea is coming up.
11. I've luckily found 40+ lovely founder friends who always support each other.

A few takeaways and lessons:
1. Take time off and get enough sleep to avoid burnout and health issues. I've barely stopped working a single day since mid-May, and I've never got sick this easily in my life. I lost my voice twice, and the second time took a month to recover, which hit a lot of my work. My mood swung up and down like a rollercoaster. My wife sometimes thought I was turning into a psycho, and I occasionally became a road rager. Taking almost 100% of last weekend off, with family time and some GTA fun, got me fully re-energised this week.
2. This path is super tough and unpredictable. Always talk to people (not bots). Saying your pain and stress out loud will calm your nerves a lot.
3. Networking is part of your business, but don't attend too many events of the same sort once you already know the drill.
4. No matter how much you've learned or heard from others, you have to put it into practice to see if it works for you and your business.
5. There's still lots of demand for service-based tech consulting, but the information gap between you and your clients keeps shrinking. Always keep one soul-searching question in mind: can the frontier models already do what your service or product does? One fun example from mine: a "chatbot + Google Drive files and spreadsheet data sync" feature I built for a client 4 months ago can now be 90%+ achieved out of the box by personal agents like Muse AI.
6. Traditional small businesses are not a great ICP. They have very limited time to collab and limited budget to pay. If possible, try enterprises instead.
7. Price wisely. I didn't do this well, and I'm still learning and optimising.
8. Opportunities are in the areas you've been dodging all along.
9. If you can't find your people, build your own group.
10. Be focused! One big problem for me was trying to do too many things at once. In the end, many of them didn't move an inch.
11. If you're still working full time and hustling on the side, don't go all in until your side hustle already brings in enough stable revenue.

To wrap up, the answer: the most expensive part of running my business in the first 6 months was FINES!!! One tow-away, charged by both the towing company and the city council, plus two speeding tickets, both on my way to meet potential clients. A final takeaway from that trauma: as an early-stage founder, take public transport as much as possible. 🤣
