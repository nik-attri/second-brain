Your rank tracker might be lying to you right now. Here’s what to do.
If your keyword positions looked strange over the past few weeks, you may not be imagining it.
Semrush has now confirmed that Google is returning inaccurate results to some automated rank-tracking tools, and says it is an industry-wide problem. Ahrefs, AccuRanker and BrightLocal reported similar issues, going back to September. searchenginelandsearchengineland
The scale varies by tool. DemandSphere flagged about 6.7% of collected SERPs as suspected poisoning on Oct 23, and SEOmonitor reported incorrect data on roughly 10% of tracked keywords between Oct 9 and 13. Some providers think Google’s efforts to restrict large-scale automated data collection may be the reason, but Google hasn’t confirmed this. searchenginelandsearchengineland
Why this matters:
After a spam update, many of us open the rank tracker first. If that data is unreliable, we can make the wrong call: rewriting pages that were never hurt, or celebrating a “recovery” that didn’t happen.
What I do instead:
1. Treat GSC as the primary source. It shows real impressions, clicks and average position from actual searches, not scraped results.
2. Use the tracker for trends, not single numbers. One day’s drop means nothing. A consistent movement across tools and GSC means something.
3. Cross-check suspicious drops manually. Search the keyword yourself (logged out, correct location) before reporting a loss to a client.
4. Annotate and wait. Note the date, compare against GSC clicks, and avoid sitewide changes based on tracker data alone.
5. Tell clients early. A short note saying “tool data is currently noisy; we’re validating with Search Console” builds more trust than a panicked report.
SEO tool scores and rank numbers are useful signals, but they are not Google’s own data. When they disagree with GSC, I trust GSC.
