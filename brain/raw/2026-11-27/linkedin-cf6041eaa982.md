THE BIGGEST MISTAKE A SUCCESSFUL COMPANY CAN MAKE? CONFUSING A WELL-RUN BUSINESS WITH A BUSINESS THAT DOESN’T NEED ITS PEOPLE.

When a company dominates its market, operates efficiently, and continues delivering results even during disruptive periods, somebody is doing something very right.

Systems don’t magically maintain themselves.
Infrastructure doesn’t protect itself.
Operations don’t run on PowerPoint presentations.
And complex businesses don’t stay competitive because executives have impressive titles.

It’s the people behind the scenes.

The experienced professionals who understand the architecture, the dependencies, the risks, the history, the workarounds, and, most importantly, WHY things work the way they do.

Here’s where upper management sometimes gets dangerously short-sighted.

They start looking at the expense column instead of the bigger picture.

They see salaries as costs rather than investments in institutional knowledge, operational resilience, and business continuity.

They start downsizing the very people responsible for making the business look successful.

And then something interesting happens.

The business starts breaking in places nobody anticipated.

Problems that were once resolved quietly become executive escalations. Systems that appeared effortless suddenly require expensive intervention. Decisions that once took minutes now take weeks.

And eventually, the REAL decision-makers start asking:

“What the hell is going on here?”

Well, here’s what’s going on.

You eliminated the very people who understood how the machine actually worked.

And here’s the irony:

Some executives looked like brilliant business leaders precisely because they had brilliant people underneath them quietly making the right decisions, solving problems, preventing disasters, and keeping the entire operation moving.

You didn’t necessarily create operational excellence.

You inherited it.

And now you’re dismantling it to make a quarterly spreadsheet look better.

Let me tell you something:

There isn’t a metric sophisticated enough to accurately measure the value of all the institutional knowledge, experience, relationships, and intelligence that keeps a dominant enterprise running.

You can calculate payroll savings.

You can calculate headcount reductions.

But can you calculate the cost of everything those people prevented from going wrong?

Can you measure the value of 20 years of experience walking out the door?

Can you put a price on knowing exactly where the bodies are buried in an enterprise infrastructure?

Apparently, some management teams think they can.

Good luck with that.

You wanted to look smart by cutting costs. Congratulations. Now show everyone how smart you are when the people who made you look smart are gone.
