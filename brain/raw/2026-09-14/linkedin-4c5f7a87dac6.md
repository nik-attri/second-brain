AI can hand you every option, but you still need to make the judgement

One of my earliest bosses and mentors used to tell me I had "good judgement." This concept of judgement seemed tricky to explain, even if we could identify the examples of when good judgement was applied. We'd talk about it often: how do you actually get “good judgement”? How do you know if someone has it, or doesn't? Can you build it in someone who's still figuring it out? We never landed on a definitive answer. 

Others have though! In 2020 published in Harvard Business Review, Sir Andrew Likierman interviewed CEOs, senior partners, generals, doctors, scientists, priests, and diplomats to work out what good judgement is made of. He found six components: learning, trust, experience, detachment, options, and delivery. And he makes the case explicitly for why it matters — judgement is what lets you make a sound call when there's no clear-cut data and no obvious path.

That's a good description of more of our decisions as AI gets involved. AI is genuinely excellent at some of what feeds good judgement — it can hand you more options than you'd find yourself, pull relevant experience and precedent at a scale no person could review manually, accelerate the "learning" and "options" parts of the list. That's real, and I don't think we should undersell it (although let’s not assume it fully takes the place of doing the reading, listening and learning for ourselves).

But judgement was never about having more inputs. It's what you do with them when there's no single right answer and someone needs to still make a decision without complete information. AI also can't do trust: the trust people place in you when they follow a call you make, or the trust and confidence you have to place in your own instinct to make one. It can't do detachment when the outcome is genuinely yours to own. And it can't do delivery: standing behind a decision out loud, in the room, when it counts. My mentor understood the human side of judgement decades before anyone was worried about AI.

It's the same thing I keep circling back to in different guises: AI can't read the room. Reading the room and deciding what to do about it is just judgement, wearing another name.

Where do you go — or who do you call — when the answer isn't obviously right or wrong; good or bad; clear or murky?
