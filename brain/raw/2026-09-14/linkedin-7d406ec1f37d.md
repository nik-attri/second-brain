For years, much of the value of FP&A has come from its ability to produce answers.

What happened? Why did margin move? What happens if we increase headcount? What does the next twelve months look like if sales fall 5%?

Those answers took time. Someone had to find the data, interrogate it, build the model and run the scenarios.

McKinsey recently argued that AI agents could make continuous financial planning practical at scale, allowing organisations to identify risks sooner, evaluate trade-offs faster and continually update scenarios.

Gartner sees a similar shift towards continuous, AI-driven decision intelligence. Interestingly, its research also found that while 45% of finance AI investment is focused on productivity, only 20% is focused on improving decision quality.

That distinction feels important.

If AI can produce analysis and scenarios in minutes rather than hours or days, producing the answer becomes less valuable.

So where does the value move?

Deloitte believes finance professionals will increasingly need to apply their skills to AI-generated outputs rather than manually produced ones. Their emerging “finance athlete” applies business context to those outputs and knows when human judgement needs to take over.

Perhaps that's where FP&A becomes particularly interesting.

Knowing which question to ask, whether the assumptions make sense, what the model might be missing, which scenario is actually plausible.
And ultimately, what the business should do.

The ability to ask better questions, understand the context behind the numbers and recognise when an answer doesn't quite make sense.
