I used to think I was practically bulletproof when it came to corporate  restructurings.
Give me an organisational chart, a spreadsheet and legislation of the jurisdiction, and I can build you a load bearing bridge from A to B.

No drama. No sentiment. Just commercial rationale, procedural fairness and a suspiciously colour coded Excel spreadsheet.

Lawyers have a rather peculiar superpower.
We can turn someone's career into an organisational structure, call it a business rationale and explain it with a perfectly straight face.

We separate. We restructure. We protect the corporate client. We know precisely which paragraph to refer to when someone challenges the process.
And feelings? Well, feelings aren't exactly listed as a mandatory annexure

Or so I thought.

Recently, I sat across from a young woman who was bright, ambitious and genuinely excited about her career.

And there I was, with my carefully prepared notes, explaining that her position was disappearing.

The only reason was  because the  numbers upstairs had apparently decided they were going on a diet.

I had the process mapped out.
What I didn't have was a spreadsheet formula for watching someone's face change as they realised the career they were building was coming to an end.

For someone who can argue a procedural point until the other side needs a lunch break, that day I was .... 

I know I cannot cry at every retrenchment consultation. I would  need a therapist on retainer and a separate billing code for emotional damages. I am a commercial lawyer and I know I have a reserved spot in a very well organised corner of hell. 

But that consultation stayed with me.
Because the person sitting across from me wasn't  a headcount, a salary cost or an 'affected employee'.
She was a person with a mortgage, ambitions, responsibilities and family commitments

She woke up that morning expecting an ordinary day at work, not a consultation that would change the trajectory of her life.
And that is the part we don't always see in the boardroom.

I have  learnt that not every problem can be solved with legislation, and not every perfectly lawful outcome feels remotely fair to the person living through it.

Legislation gives us the framework. Commercial reality gives us the reason. But humanity must be present . 

You can build the most technically sound bridge in the world, but never forget that there are real people standing on the other side.
