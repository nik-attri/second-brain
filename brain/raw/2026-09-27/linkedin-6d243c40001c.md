68% of financial institutions are already testing agentic AI for fraud — and most of those pilots are quietly failing. The reason is more mundane than the hype suggests: the AI models run isolated from real-time transaction data, generating insight without ever having full context on the actual case.
Feedzai just launched Farol, an AI agent embedded directly into its RiskOps Studio, aimed at that exact gap — built into existing risk workflows with full data context and, notably, transparent audit trails rather than a black-box output.
What it does:
Risk strategy — flags ineffective rules, recommends sharper thresholds; cuts rule management from days to minutes
Investigations — summarizes alert data for analysts, claiming a 20% reduction in alert handling time
Knowledge — an embedded product expert inside the platform
SAR drafting — automates the information compilation behind Suspicious Activity Reports, up to 12x faster
Pedro Barata, Feedzai's CPO, put it plainly: the agent "puts actionable intelligence directly in the hands of the people fighting fraud every day."
Here's what I'd flag to any bank or fintech evaluating this category of tool, and it's not the speed claim — it's the governance question sitting underneath it:
An agent that auto-drafts SARs and recommends new detection rules is now a decision-maker in a regulated process, not a productivity tool. That reframes what "deploying" it actually requires:
Model risk management, not just IT procurement — a rule threshold the agent recommends and a SAR it drafts both need the same validation, testing, and sign-off discipline as a model built in-house. "It's a vendor product" doesn't exempt it from that.
Traceable decision trails, by design — IDC noted this category succeeds because it removes manual data assembly. But every rule change or SAR the agent produces has to be reconstructable months later: what data it saw, what logic it applied, who approved the output. That's not a feature request — it's what ISO 42001's AI management system requirements are built to enforce.
Human accountability stays explicit — "the agent recommended it" cannot become the answer when a regulator asks why a SAR was filed, or wasn't. Governance has to define exactly where human sign-off sits in that workflow, before deployment, not after an incident.
This is the exact pairing we build for banking and fintech clients at Vexler CPA's & Capital Advisors: treating agentic fraud tools as a governed AI system under ISO 42001, with antifraud controls and audit trail requirements designed in from day one — not bolted on once a regulator asks the first hard question.
Before adopting a tool like this: does your model risk framework already cover agentic AI vendors, or does "vendor tool" still mean "outside our governance scope"?
