We spent the last few years asking:
“What can AI do?”
I think the more important question now is:
“What should AI be allowed to do?”

What boundaries have you set for AI at your organization? What can it access, what needs human sign-off, and what’s off-limits?

Recent incidents involving Anthropic’s Claude pushes us to question ourselves NOW.
In one case, attackers manipulated Claude Code and used it to automate large parts of a cyber-espionage operation targeting organizations including government agencies.
More recently, during cybersecurity evaluations, Claude was placed in what it was told was a simulated environment. But it could actually reach the real internet, and it ended up gaining unauthorized access to real organizations’ systems, in most cases believing they were part of the exercise.
That distinction matters.

As AI moves from answering questions → to using tools → to executing workflows → to acting autonomously, the consequences of poorly defined boundaries become much bigger.
This is where the EU AI Act comes in.
The Act isn’t simply about restricting AI. It introduces risk management, transparency, human oversight, cybersecurity and responsibility, particularly for powerful general-purpose AI models.
And it has teeth. Companies that break the rules can face fines of up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover, whichever is higher. Since August, the EU can also fine providers of general-purpose AI models directly.
But it’s still a work in progress. The strictest rules for high-risk AI have been pushed back to late 2027 and 2028. And the Act was largely designed around AI systems that make decisions, not AI agents that take actions on their own. An agent wandering outside its test environment into real systems is exactly the kind of case the rules are still catching up with.
I work in compliance for fintechs. Our whole job is making powerful systems trustworthy through boundaries, audit trails and accountability.
We already have rules that help humans coexist.
Traffic rules don’t stop us from driving.
Financial regulations don’t stop us from moving money.
Building codes don’t stop us from constructing skyscrapers.
They create boundaries that allow powerful systems to operate safely.
AI may need the same social infrastructure.
We need to start defining:
• What decisions can an AI make independently?
• What systems should it be allowed to access?
• When must a human remain in the loop?
• Who is responsible when an autonomous agent causes harm?
• And perhaps most importantly: how does an AI know when it has crossed a boundary it cannot physically see?
The next phase of AI development may not just be about building more capable models.
It may be about developing the rules that allow humans and increasingly autonomous AI systems to live and work together.
