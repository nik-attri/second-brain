Martin Siame asks,

“Is it okay to plant onions directly if you can't manage to transplant? How does it perform?”

It is okay. And it can perform.

Most onion farmers, mid to small scale, in Kenya first raise seedlings in a nursery before transplanting them into the main field.

That is what we do.

But not every onion farmer starts with a nursery.

Some sow the seeds directly into the main field through direct seeding.

In fact, some of the largest onion farms in the world do not transplant onions at all. They direct-seed the entire field.

Why would they do that?

First, it saves labour. Everything that happens before transplanting is eliminated.

Second, the onions never experience transplant shock because they germinate where they will eventually mature.

It can be an excellent system.

But only under the right conditions.

Third, Controlling weeds becomes a much bigger challenge. Weeds in a nursery are already difficult enough. Now imagine managing weeds across 100 acres of nursery conditions.

Fourth, uniform germination also becomes critical. Some of our favourite seeds have very poor germination rates. 

Fifth, direct seeding is difficult to do manually because asking human sowers to maintain consistent seed spacing and depth is not easy, adding the fact that an onion seed is the size of a grain of sand. 

Sixth, Irrigation during the first few weeks must also be precise. Even a heavy downpour can wash away seeds.

Finally, you need mechanizable land, flat open plains with sandy loam soil work best. Black cotton will not suffice. 

Still, direct seeding has its allure.

Especially when you think about skipping the drama of transplanting hundreds of thousands of seedlings.

But, it is difficult to do manually.

So farmers growing onions at scale use mechanical vacuum seeders to achieve the uniformity they need.

For us, transplanting remains the preferred method. Mostly due to financial limitations. 

But I must admit, the thought of skipping transplanting drama is becoming harder to ignore.
