We don’t have a technology problem. We have a synchronization problem.

Look at land record management in Pakistan.

Punjab has invested in digitization through initiatives such as e-Patwari, PLRA and related digital land record services. Sindh has pursued its own digitization and modernization efforts. Different departments, boards, development authorities and local bodies have their own systems, databases, workflows and — often — their own definitions of the same piece of information.

On paper, this sounds like progress.

But then comes the moment when these systems actually need to talk to each other.

A land parcel needs to be verified.
Ownership needs to be matched.
A GPC needs to be reconciled with a revenue record.
A mutation needs to reflect somewhere else.
A development authority needs information from the revenue department.
A citizen expects one answer from the government — not five different answers from five databases.

And suddenly, we are back to square one.

The technology gets blamed.

"The system isn't working."
"The software has an issue."
"The database isn't compatible."
"Let's build another portal."

But perhaps the real problem is not the software.

It is the mindset behind the software.

We have historically built government systems in silos — department by department, project by project, jurisdiction by jurisdiction.

Digitizing a silo does not eliminate the silo.

It simply creates a digital silo.

The real transformation begins when we stop asking:

«"How do I digitize my department?"»

and start asking:

«"How does the government operate as one connected system?"»

Land is a perfect example.

A parcel should have a consistent digital identity.
Its ownership, mutations, survey information, GPC, spatial boundaries, taxation, development status and relevant transactions should be able to connect through agreed standards and interoperable platforms — while respecting jurisdiction, authority and data governance.

Punjab and Sindh don't necessarily need identical systems.

But they do need systems that can synchronize where synchronization is required.

Because interoperability is not a feature that you bolt on at the end of a project.

It is an architectural decision.

Until we change that mindset, we will continue spending millions digitizing individual processes, only to discover that when two systems need to exchange information, someone still has to print a form, carry a file, verify a record and manually enter the same information again.

That isn't digital transformation.

That's digitizing the paperwork — and preserving the silo.

Pakistan doesn't just need more digital systems.

We need connected digital government.

And perhaps the most important transformation isn't technological at all.

It's learning to stop building systems for departments and start building ecosystems for citizens.
