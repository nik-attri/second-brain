Jeremy D. Stick  Ph.D. runs HR on a philosophy he knows some people will challenge: develop people so they could leave, and treat them so well that they stay.
 
Jeremy is the CHRO at DaBella, a home improvement company of 2,600 people across 68 offices in 28 states.
 
How he leads:
 
→ He measures the turnover that actually hurts. Departures get split into the ones you can absorb and the ones you truly regret, and the math goes to leadership: three months to backfill, three to train, six months of the work landing on your best people. Retaining bad employees is a cost, he says. Losing good ones is a bigger one.
 
→ He takes the fear out of benefits decisions. For the employee scared of a $3,000 deductible, he opens a spreadsheet: the deductible budgeted into an HSA, the tax break, what 3% in a 401(k) compounds into. People stop deciding on emotion once they can see the numbers. It's the education he never got, back when his own answer to the 401(k) was "I want my money and I want it now."
 
→ He'll tell you about the one that spiraled. At a previous company, a wellness stipend meant for physical and mental health drifted into a catchall for anything that felt good, and walking it back meant taking something away from people. His verdict: great plan, terrible execution.
 
And he defends the profession itself. When people wave off the humans behind the title as just "HR," his answer is an article he wrote: "My name is Jeremy, not HR." People in this profession have names, he says. They're here to help, and they're not the enemy.
 
If you've ever been called "HR" more often than your own name, this one's for you.
