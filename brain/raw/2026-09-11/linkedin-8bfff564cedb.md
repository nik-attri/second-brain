AI may not be the biggest cybersecurity risk.

Our assumption about what AI can automate might be.

We are watching AI perform tasks that seemed impossible a few years ago.

Write software.
Analyze contracts.
Generate reports.
Build applications.
Automate workflows.
Make decisions.
Operate across multiple systems.

And the natural question from business leaders is becoming:
“Why are we still doing this manually?”

That is the right question.
But there is another question executives need to ask:
“What happens when an attacker gets the same capability?”

This is where the conversation becomes uncomfortable.
Cyberattacks have traditionally required people to perform hundreds of individual tasks.

Reconnaissance.
Research.
Vulnerability discovery.
Credential attacks.
Social engineering.
Exploit development.
Data discovery.
Exfiltration.

AI has the potential to compress many of these activities into highly automated workflows.

Recent threat intelligence is already highlighting activity ranging from Russian espionage and ShinyHunters' data theft operations, to exploit foundries and autonomous attack frameworks, attacks reaching into the AI supply chain, and hacktivist targeting of European political entities.
The danger is not necessarily an AI that independently launches a catastrophic attack tomorrow.

The danger is an attacker who can turn a 10-person operation into the operational equivalent of hundreds of automated agents.
That changes the economics of cybercrime.
And this is where many organizations may be underprepared.
We are aggressively investing in AI to remove friction from our businesses.

But are we investing equally in removing friction from our security operations?
Can we detect compromised identities quickly enough?
Can we contain an automated attack before it moves laterally?
Can we identify malicious activity generated at machine speed?
Can our privileged-access controls withstand automated credential abuse?
Can we trace what an AI agent accessed, changed or transmitted?
Can our incident-response process operate at the speed of an AI-enabled attack?

These are no longer theoretical architecture questions.
They are becoming board-level risk questions.
The AI race is accelerating.
The organizations that succeed will not be those that automate the most.
They will be those that understand what must be automated, what must remain controlled, and where human authorization must still exist.

AI can become one of the greatest productivity multipliers of our generation.
It can also become a force multiplier for cyberattacks.
The difference will be determined by how seriously we design for the second possibility.

The question for every executive should be:
“If AI can automate our business, how much of our attack surface can it automate too?”

That answer deserves as much attention as the AI strategy itself.
