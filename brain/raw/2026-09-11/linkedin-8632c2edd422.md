Enterprise solutioning is not about finding a way to fit your platform everywhere.

Consider a large enterprise with multiple ERPs, e-invoicing platforms, workflows, interfaces, and country-specific processes.

The easiest approach is often:

“How do we connect all of this to our platform?”

But that may be the wrong question.

Suppose only 2% of invoices originate from a particular channel, while integrating that channel requires significant development, testing, customer effort, and ongoing maintenance.

Should we still integrate it simply because our platform can?

Not necessarily.

The better question is:

“Is the business value of changing this flow greater than the cost and complexity of leaving it as it is?”

This is where enterprise solutioning becomes much more than architecture diagrams and integration patterns.

Customers care about cost, compliance, operational continuity, implementation effort, risk, and change management.

And there is another dimension we often underestimate: customer-side effort.

If implementing our solution requires the customer to modify several upstream systems, allocate multiple teams, build interfaces, perform extensive testing, and change established processes, that effort is part of the solution cost — even if it doesn’t appear in our SOW.

A technically elegant solution can therefore be a commercially poor solution.

Sometimes the right architecture is to integrate.

Sometimes it is to coexist.

Sometimes it is to automate only the high-value flows.

And sometimes the smartest recommendation is simply:

“Don’t change this part. The economics don’t justify it.”

That may mean selling slightly less today.

But it also tells the customer something important:

We are solving their problem, not looking for places to install our product.

Great solutioning isn’t about maximizing the footprint of your platform.

It’s about maximizing the value of the change you are asking the customer to make.
