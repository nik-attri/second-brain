TIME just named McKinsey & Company the #1 company for future leaders, four years running.
The paradox: in my view, most consultants make poor operators. And yet I emphatically agree with the ranking. The Firm is the ultimate leadership factory.
As a McKinsey partner who moved into operating roles, here is where the real world is much harder than advising:
• Street smarts: Data only takes you so far; models can be wrong. And people are "political animals," as Aristotle put it. Politics needs to be managed; agendas need to be understood.
• Grit: The real world is tough as hell. Legacy culture, process and technology change slowly from the inside. Some days it feels like Sisyphus pushing the boulder up the mountain.
• Humility: Being the smartest person in the room can be a handicap. The people who know the business best may never have built a PowerPoint slide; they may not wear starched collars. But they are smarter than you.
• Being in the weeds: You can't understand a business at a "high level." Details matter.
• Owning the decision: Even the right decision can turn out wrong. When it does, you own it.
So why does McKinsey & Company keep topping these lists? Because of the essential, timeless leadership skills the Firm drills into its people:
• Problem-solving rigor: Hypothesis-driven, backed by data. Math, creativity, synthesis. Left brain and right brain, both needed.
• Client engagement: Listen, understand motivations, build trust, put the client first; Servant leadership.
• People leadership: You lead people by challenging them, caring about them and helping them develop. Inspiration beats authority. Trust, respect and "followership," as we call it at the Firm, are hard to earn.
• Pristine communication: An idea that isn't perfectly communicated risks staying in your head, or in a spreadsheet, and never moving into action.
Then the Firm's culture, the softer elements:
• Collaboration: you succeed when everyone around you succeeds.
• Challenge: Relentless, honest, constructive two-way feedback. Every day, all day, with everyone.
• Up or out: if you aren't continuously progressing, you leave.
• Non-hierarchical, with an obligation to dissent. In problem solving, partners and early-tenure associates are on a level playing field.
• Confidence and poise, in the face of chaos and ambiguity.
To have experienced that culture early in my career was priceless. McKinsey gave me the foundation. Operating in the trenches taught me how to apply it. Although some days I still feel like a first-year associate.
Grateful to the Firm for that start, and glad we've stayed in touch since I left.
In the spirit of the Obligation to Dissent: challenge me if you think I'm wrong, or would express it differently.
