I made an assisted rescue on Labor Day—please read this before watching the video. There’s a deeper lesson here for LinkedIN (and a warning: some swearing in the heat of the moment!).
I keep replaying those seven minutes. It was a stark reminder of how split-second decisions and real-world conditions cross over into everything we do—whether in our day jobs, leadership roles, or advocacy work.
Quisset Harbor wasn't our original plan. We arrived at MBL Stony Beach at sunrise for a 16-mile trip to Tarpaulin Cove, but the sea was rough and wind fierce. As eleven of us stood on shore, our lead said, "It's not the day we planned, but it's the day we got."
Three seasoned kayakers opted out. Eight launched, but a mile out, our lead called it off. Waves were cresting over four feet, making the Woods Hole crossing an unacceptable risk. We regrouped with all eleven and pivoted to a protected harbor instead.
Out near "The Knob," we zigged and zagged in and out of the harbor to play in the waves. I was riding waves alongside another paddler from our group when I caught his flipped kayak out of the corner of my eye and paddled over to assist. In the chaos, I called out to another paddler for help—questioning myself briefly. But he rightly held back to give us space, as trained, to prevent collisions in rough water.
It wasn't textbook. It was messy, required choice language, and took two attempts. Over lunch, he mentioned: "I wasn't really sure what you were telling me to do."
Why wasn't he sure? That miscommunication fell on both of us.
He had high-end gear. Our group assumed his equipment reflected experience. In reality, he was new, in rough surf for the first time, and had zero rescue training.
My responsibility was how I delivered directions. I fell into a common trap: assuming he knew what to do simply because I knew. I wasn't as clear as I needed to be.
Still, I'm proud of how it played out. My confidence to jump in, my years on the water, and my rescue training paid off—even if I felt like a duck: calm on the surface, paddling like hell underneath.
Leadership parallels:
Get a hobby (where you get humbled). Step away from spreadsheets. Nothing sharpens leadership faster than an unscripted pop quiz in 2- 3 foot waves.
Respect conditions. When plans fall apart, pivot quickly.
Never judge capability by appearance. Don't assume skills based on gear.
Focus on outcomes over perfection. A successful rescue just needs to work (without bypassing process).
Lead through clear direction. In high-stakes moments, guide clearly and bring others along.
When conditions get rough, pivot and execute an assisted rescue together. Support the newcomer, back your leadership, and help the team succeed.
