Are we watching an AI bubble or are some people living in a bubble about what AI can already do?

“AI will disappear when the hype dies.”

Before saying that, ask a founder:

How many junior developers are you hiring?
How much routine design work are you outsourcing?
How many people do you need for first-line support?
Which vacancies are you choosing not to refill?

Those answers matter as much as the layoff headlines.

AI doesn’t have to replace an entire profession to change its economics.

It can take over enough tasks that a business changes its next hiring decision.

1. SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT

Implementation, tests, documentation, coding and debugging are all areas where teams are already using AI.

The question is whether the same team can deliver more and whether that changes how many additional developers it hires.

Junior developers also need those tasks to learn. What replaces that training ground?

2. DESIGN AND CREATIVE WORK

AI-assisted work extends beyond basic social posts: image editing, compositing, campaign concepts, and creative variations.

Advanced design still needs taste, consistency, usability, and direction.

But if production becomes faster, clients may change what they’re willing to pay for and teams may change who they hire.

3. BPO AND CUSTOMER SUPPORT

Voice agents can be configured to answer routine questions, manage bookings, and route requests.

Human support remains essential for difficult cases.

Still, moving routine calls to software can change staffing, training, and the economics of outsourced support.

4. CONTENT AND BACK-OFFICE WORK

First drafts.
Transcription.
Document extraction.
Data entry.
Routine reporting.

For work built around repeatable outputs, “AI isn’t perfect” is an incomplete response.

The business comparison includes human errors, review costs, and the quality required for that particular task.

There is an early hiring signal, too.

Stanford and ADP’s August 2026 research found a roughly 19% employment shortfall for US workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations, relative to less-exposed peers.

The researchers found no widespread economy-wide displacement and did not establish that AI caused the gap. The adjustment appeared mainly through reduced hiring.

That deserves attention not a victory lap.

Some AI companies may be overvalued. Some products may fail.

Useful capabilities can survive both.

My bet: we’re early in the redesign of work. The effects will differ by role, company, and country.

If you hire developers, designers, or support staff:

Has AI changed the number or the experience level of people you plan to hire?
