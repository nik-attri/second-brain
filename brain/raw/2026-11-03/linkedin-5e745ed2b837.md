Discharge sounds so simple until you’re the nurse actually trying to make it happen.

“The patient is discharged.”

Perfect.

Except their ride is two hours away.

Pharmacy hasn’t filled the prescription.

The doctor forgot one order.

The family suddenly has fourteen new questions.

The patient wants to shower first.

Transport is nowhere to be found.

And now somebody is asking why the bed isn’t empty yet.

Because apparently saying “discharge” is supposed to make a human being instantly disappear.

Management sees a bed.

The nurse sees a patient who still needs instructions, meds explained, IV removed, belongings gathered, follow-up reviewed, paperwork signed, questions answered, and somehow safely moved out of the building.

And God help you if they remember one more concern right when the wheelchair arrives.

“Actually, I’ve been having chest pain since this morning.”

Fantastic.

We were so close.

Discharges are one of those hospital things that look efficient on a spreadsheet and turn into complete nonsense in real life.

Everybody wants turnover.

Everybody wants the room.

Everybody wants the next patient upstairs.

Meanwhile the nurse is trying to make sure the person leaving actually understands what the hell they’re supposed to do when they get home.

Because “bed available” is important.

But so is not sending somebody out the door confused, unprepared, and back in the ER six hours later.

A discharge is not just:

“Okay, bye.”

Although some days I really wish it was.
