𝗦𝗮𝘆𝗶𝗻𝗴 ‘𝗦𝗼𝗿𝗿𝘆’ 𝗜𝘀𝗻’𝘁 𝗘𝗺𝗽𝗮𝘁𝗵𝘆. 𝗜𝘁’𝘀 𝗝𝘂𝘀𝘁 𝗦𝘆𝗺𝗽𝗮𝘁𝗵𝘆.

We’ve all seen the flashy tech demos. A customer types an angry message into a chat window. The vendor’s shiny new LLM agent detects negative sentiment and instantly responds:

“I am so sorry to hear you are frustrated. I understand how upsetting this must be.”

The crowd applauds. Another victory for "AI empathy."

But let's pause. The agent didn't demonstrate empathy. It generated a sympathetic response based on patterns in language. The market is increasingly treating sympathy and empathy as the same thing. They're not. And unless we're clearer about the difference, we'll create confusion for organisations and disappointing experiences for customers.

Sympathy is passive. It's the linguistic equivalent of a Hallmark card. It acknowledges pain but doesn't change the customer's situation.
Empathy is different. It's an active workflow.

Customers don't contact support because they want a digital entity to feel sorry for them. They want their problem solved. True empathy requires understanding the impact of an issue and taking action to resolve it.
Real empathy follows a simple five-step workflow:

1️⃣ Listen - Giving the customer space to lay out the issue without interruption.
2️⃣ Validate - Telling the customer exactly what you heard and defining the problem from their perspective.
3️⃣ Commit - Stating clearly what you are going to do about it right now to set transparent expectations.
4️⃣ Execute - Doing the heavy lifting and solving the root issue.
5️⃣ Feedback - Closing the circle by telling the customer exactly what has been done.

You can't automate a workflow you haven't mastered manually. Before handing empathy to an AI agent, organisations need to build and refine these workflows with their human teams.

For service leaders, the challenge isn't teaching employees to listen empathetically. Most can learn that quickly. The real test is execution. Leaders must remove the barriers that prevent frontline teams from solving problems: provide access to information, simplify approvals, clarify decision rights, and create effective escalation paths.

Just as importantly, they need the influence to tackle root causes wherever they exist, whether in operations, technology, policy, or elsewhere in the business. Without that support, employees can empathise with customers but remain powerless to help them.

Let's leave the sympathy to Hallmark.

If we want to build the future of CX, let's stop optimising for better apology scripts and start building workflows that actually solve customer problems.
