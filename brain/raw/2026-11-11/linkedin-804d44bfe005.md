Just because a process is automated does not mean it has been improved.
Organisations are increasingly investing in technology to streamline operations, reduce costs, and improve productivity.
But there is a question I believe deserves more attention:
Are we improving the way we work, or simply making inefficient processes run faster?
Imagine an organisation where employees must obtain five different approvals before processing a simple customer request.
The organisation introduces a digital system that automatically routes the request through all five approval stages.
The process is now faster. Paperwork has been reduced, and employees no longer need to chase approvals manually.
Sounds like progress, doesn't it?
But here's the question: Were all five approvals necessary in the first place?
If only two approvals were genuinely required, the organisation has invested in technology to automate three unnecessary steps.
It has made the process faster without necessarily making it better.
This is where the distinction between process automation and process improvement becomes important.
Process automation focuses on using technology to perform existing activities with less manual intervention.
Process improvement examines whether those activities are necessary, efficient, valuable, and aligned with business objectives.
Automation asks: How can we do this faster?
Process improvement asks: Why are we doing this, and is there a better way?
Both are valuable, but they are not interchangeable.
From a Business Analysis perspective, I believe organisations should examine their processes critically before deciding what to automate.
This means understanding the current workflow, identifying bottlenecks, challenging unnecessary activities, engaging stakeholders, and defining what success should look like.
Otherwise, organisations risk digitising inefficiencies rather than eliminating them.
And the consequences may extend beyond financial costs. Employees can become frustrated, customers may continue to experience poor service, and organisations may struggle to realise the expected benefits of their technology investments.
Of course, automation itself can deliver meaningful improvements. But technology should support a well-understood business need, not substitute for understanding it.
The real measure of transformation is not how much technology an organisation introduces, but how much value the change creates.
Perhaps the most important question organisations should ask before automating any process is:
If we were designing this process from scratch today, would we still do it this way?
