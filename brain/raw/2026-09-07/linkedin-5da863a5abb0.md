13 years in software engineering has taught me one thing:

The technology changes.
The tools change.
The job titles change.

But the people who keep learning are the ones who keep moving forward.

And in 2026, that lesson has become more important than ever.

We’re seeing companies like PayPal, Uber, Oracle, Microsoft and others restructure teams and invest heavily in AI, automation, and infrastructure.

The conversation is often:

“Is AI going to take our jobs?”

I think we’re asking the wrong question.

The better question is:

👉 “What parts of my job will AI change — and am I ready for that change?”

Because the impact isn't limited to layoffs.

The bigger challenge could be what happens after a layoff.

Someone with 10–15 years of experience may suddenly find themselves competing in a market where:

• Experience alone isn't enough
• Traditional skills are becoming commodities
• Companies expect AI fluency
• Job descriptions are changing rapidly
• Salary expectations can become disconnected from years of experience

That’s uncomfortable to think about.

But I also see a huge opportunity.

💡 A developer who learns AI can become more productive.

💡 An architect who understands AI can design new kinds of systems.

💡 A business analyst who knows how to work with AI can automate entire workflows.

💡 An engineer who learns to build AI-powered products can create value that wasn’t possible a few years ago.

AI doesn't have to be our competitor.

It can become our most powerful collaborator.

For me, the goal isn't to compete with AI.

It is to become the engineer who knows how to use AI to solve bigger problems.

The next decade may not reward the people who know the most tools.

It may reward the people who are willing to keep learning, keep adapting, and keep reinventing themselves.
