I have not seen a machine for cleaning sunflower or hibiscus anywhere in Dawanau.

The cleaning is done manually, mostly by women.

I asked the manager of a warehouse handling sunflower why there was no cleaning machine for it.

He told me there is no machine for cleaning sunflower in Dawanau.

I asked the same question about hibiscus, and the manager told me the same thing.

Hmmmm.

And the explanation I was given is that these commodities are flowers, and the quality and appearance of the flower matter. There is concern that a machine designed to separate sticks and other impurities could damage or crush the flowers.

Why can’t we engineer a machine that cleans them without damaging their quality?

We talk a lot about improved seeds, fertiliser and production.

But what about the physical technology that handles what farmers have already produced?

Imagine producing an export-quality commodity and then having to clean it manually because the right machinery is not available.

And these are not insignificant commodities.

Hibiscus and sunflower move through agricultural markets and have export and industrial uses.

If quality matters, then cleaning, sorting and grading cannot just be an afterthought.

We cannot consistently meet demanding markets by simply looking at a commodity and saying, “It looks clean.”

Dear Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security , I don’t know if this particular gap is already on your radar.

But part of what I am trying to do through this fieldwork is identify these gaps and bring them to attention.

Please look into the technology around cleaning, sorting and grading these commodities.

And to Nigerians with capital:

I am not saying go and start trading sunflower or hibiscus.

I am saying look at the machinery gap.

Bring in or develop the right cleaning, sorting and grading technology and offer it as a service.

Because opportunity is not only in the commodity.

It is also in the technology that helps the commodity become valuable.
