When AI Refuses to Investigate the Very Thing We Want to Protect 🐟🔎

Sometimes, technology has a funny way of reminding us how complicated our work can be.

Back when I was working at UNESCO, I remember that identifying potentially illicit online sales of cultural objects could involve hiring experts to manually search websites and marketplaces for suspicious listings.

I was curious, so I started experimenting with browser agents myself. Instead of opening hundreds of pages one by one, why not let an AI agent help navigate the web and collect publicly available information?

After leaving UNESCO, I continued exploring the idea. I even built a small OSINT workflow using Python and Gemini through what people now call *vibe coding*—combining web search, data collection, and AI-assisted screening to identify potentially suspicious artifact listings.

The idea was simple: use technology to help investigators find signals hidden in an overwhelming amount of online information.
Fast-forward to today.

I tried running a similar search using TinyFish, looking for publicly available information about potentially illicit artifact sales across websites, marketplaces, and social media.
And then…

🚫 REQUEST FAILED. 🚫

The platform refused to process the task because it might violate its usage policies.
Well, that was unexpected. 😂

Of course, a refusal message doesn't prove that anyone is concealing information or deliberately protecting illegal traders. Different AI platforms have different safeguards, and a legitimate investigative objective can still trigger a restriction.

But it does raise an interesting question:
As AI becomes more capable of automating online investigations, how do we ensure that legitimate cultural heritage protection and law-enforcement research remain possible, responsible, and transparent?

There is a real difference between using automation to facilitate illicit trade and using it to document publicly available evidence of potentially illegal activity.

For professionals working in cultural heritage protection, law enforcement, and digital investigations, that distinction matters.

Perhaps the next challenge isn't simply building smarter agents.
It's making sure that responsible investigators can use them appropriately, with clear safeguards, reliable evidence, and human verification.

Or maybe I just need to rephrase my prompt. 😂🐟

Either way, the little fish has given me something to think about.
