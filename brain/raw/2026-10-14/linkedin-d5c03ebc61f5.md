What if a video could become the starting point for teaching a robot a new skill?Google’s demonstration of Gemini 4 Argon and Apptronik’s Apollo humanoid playing cornhole got me thinking about something bigger than the demonstration itself.The interesting part isn’t just that a robot can throw a beanbag.It’s the process behind it.Observe → Understand → Simulate → Test → Execute.A system watches an activity, infers something about the physics involved, builds a simulation, and uses that environment to develop a strategy before attempting the task in the real world.That’s a fascinating change in how we might approach robotics.Traditionally, teaching robots new physical tasks can require extensive modeling, programming, demonstrations, and iteration.If AI can help automate parts of that process, the bottleneck starts to move.The challenge becomes less about manually specifying every step and more about whether the system can build an accurate enough model of reality, learn from simulation, and transfer that learning to physical hardware.And there’s a critical caveat: simulating the physical world is not the same as perfectly reproducing it.Friction, contact, object variation, and small errors in perception can make the real world behave differently from a simulation. Reliable systems still need feedback, testing, and correction.But the broader implication is exciting.A video could become more than something an AI model describes.It could become a source of knowledge from which a physical agent learns how to act.I’m increasingly interested in this intersection of foundation models, simulation, and robotics because it expands the question we’re asking about AI.Not just:Can AI understand what it sees?But:Can it use what it sees to learn how to do something it has never done before?That’s where physical AI gets particularly interesting.#PhysicalAI #Robotics #Gemini #AIEngineering #AgenticAI #Simulation #HumanoidRobots #ArtificialIntelligence
