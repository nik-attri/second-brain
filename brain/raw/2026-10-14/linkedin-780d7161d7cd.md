Open-book costing sounds wonderfully objective.

The supplier shows you material, labour, overhead, logistics, depreciation and margin. Procurement checks the numbers, challenges a few assumptions, and eventually arrives at a “fair” price.

But there is one question the spreadsheet cannot answer:
How much does the supplier actually deserve to earn?

Suppose the cost base is completely transparent and the supplier asks for an 8% margin.

Is that too much?
Maybe they are making a commodity part with little risk and plenty of competition. Maybe they invested €10 million in dedicated capacity for you. Maybe your forecasts are terrible, engineering changes constantly and half their organisation is dealing with your exceptions. Maybe they own a process nobody else can currently reproduce.

The cost model can tell you what the product costs them.
It cannot tell you what return is reasonable for the risk, capability, capital and inconvenience they are taking on.

And I think procurement sometimes hides behind the apparent precision of open-book calculations because that second conversation is harder.
We challenge whether a machine hour should cost €72 or €68, while quietly accepting that the “right” margin is whatever percentage somebody put into the model.

There is no universally fair supplier margin.
The useful question is what return this supplier needs to make your business attractive relative to everything else they could do with the same people, machines and capital.

You can still negotiate the hell out of it.
But at least then you're negotiating economics, not pretending the spreadsheet has already decided what is fair.
