HOW IS THAT BIOLOGICALLY EVEN POSSIBLE? 🔬 | EPISODE 01

🦎 The animal that can rebuild its own limbs.

If a human loses an arm, the body attempts to close the wound. The result is not a new arm, but repaired tissue and a permanent loss of function.

Yet the axolotl can regenerate an entire limb — including muscle, bone, blood vessels and nerves.

How?

And more importantly, what is stopping our bodies from doing the same?

One of the most fascinating aspects of regeneration is that the axolotl doesn't simply grow replacement tissue at random. Its cells participate in a coordinated biological process.

Following an injury, cells near the wound help form a structure called a blastema — a population of proliferating cells that contributes to rebuilding the missing tissues.

Think of it as a biological construction site.

But unlike a conventional construction site, there is no external blueprint, no engineer directing each cell and no central controller manually assigning tasks.

Instead, cells respond to molecular signals, communicate with surrounding tissues and activate gene-regulatory programmes that help coordinate regeneration.

Growth factors, signalling pathways and changes in gene expression all contribute to determining what happens next.

The extraordinary part isn't simply that the axolotl can produce new cells.

It's that those cells can help reconstruct organised anatomy.

A limb is not just a collection of cells. Its tissues must develop in the correct proportions, connect appropriately and restore functional structures.

Regeneration therefore raises a much deeper question than how animals heal.

How does a living organism coordinate thousands of cellular decisions to rebuild something as structurally complex as a limb?

And could understanding these mechanisms eventually help us improve human tissue repair?

Human beings possess some regenerative abilities, but our responses vary considerably between tissues and are often limited by scarring and other biological constraints. Scientists are investigating the mechanisms behind these differences.

We are not yet able to regenerate a complete human limb. But studying organisms that can do so may reveal principles that could inform future regenerative medicine.

I'm particularly interested in how molecular signalling and gene regulation determine what cells become after an injury.

Here's the question I'd like to put to researchers and fellow science enthusiasts:

If we can identify the molecular programmes that allow an axolotl to regenerate an organised limb, what do you think is the biggest barrier to translating that knowledge into human regenerative medicine — cellular identity, tissue organisation, immune responses, or something else?

I'd be interested to hear your perspectives.
