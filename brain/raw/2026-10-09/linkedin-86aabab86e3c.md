𝗜 𝗥𝘂𝗻 𝗮𝗻 𝗜𝗧 𝗖𝗼𝗻𝘀𝘂𝗹𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗖𝗼𝗺𝗽𝗮𝗻𝘆. 𝗔𝗻𝗱 𝗜 𝗗𝗼𝗻’𝘁 𝗪𝗮𝗻𝘁 𝗜𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗕𝗲 𝗔𝗜-𝗙𝗶𝗿𝘀𝘁.

It feels almost strange to write that in 2026, especially because AI is everywhere in my work. I brainstorm with it, challenge my thinking, use it to structure ideas, analyze information and take care of work I no longer see any reason to do manually. I genuinely love what it makes possible. But paradoxically, the more I use AI, the less interested I become in the idea of building an “AI-first” company.

Perhaps that comes from spending more than 20 years across sales, marketing and project management. You live through enough technological waves and waves in general, so eventually notice the same pattern: a powerful new technology arrives, everyone becomes fascinated by what it can do, and for a while we start reorganizing problems around the solution rather than the other way around. I see a version of that happening with AI now. Conversations begin with “Where can we use AI?” when I think the more important question is: 𝗪𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗮𝗿𝗲 𝘄𝗲 𝗮𝗰𝘁𝘂𝗮𝗹𝗹𝘆 𝘁𝗿𝘆𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗼 𝗳𝗶𝘅?

Stay with that question long enough and the conversation often changes completely. The company convinced it needs AI discovers it actually has a process problem. The team asking for another platform realizes it already has too many systems that don’t communicate. The workflow everyone wants to automate turns out to be something nobody can quite explain why they’re doing in the first place. Sometimes AI really is the right answer 𝗯𝘂𝘁 𝗯𝘆 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝘄𝗲’𝘃𝗲 𝗮𝗿𝗿𝗶𝘃𝗲𝗱 𝗮𝘁 𝗔𝗜 𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗿 𝘁𝗵𝗮𝗻 𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗿𝘁𝗲𝗱 𝘄𝗶𝘁𝗵 𝗶𝘁.

That distinction matters because my job isn’t to bring more technology into a company simply because more technology exists. It is to understand where the business is today, where it genuinely wants to go and what is standing in the way, and then find the most sensible route between those points. Sometimes that means sophisticated AI. Sometimes automation, better integration or a redesigned process. And sometimes the smartest solution is surprisingly boring. I’m perfectly comfortable with boring if it solves the right problem.

There is little value in using extraordinary technology to make an unnecessary process run faster, putting AI on top of disconnected systems, or automating something nobody stopped to question. AI gives us an extraordinary number of possibilities, but 𝗺𝗼𝗿𝗲 𝗽𝗼𝘀𝘀𝗶𝗯𝗶𝗹𝗶𝘁𝗶𝗲𝘀 𝗱𝗼𝗻’𝘁 𝗿𝗲𝗺𝗼𝘃𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗻𝗲𝗲𝗱 𝗳𝗼𝗿 𝗷𝘂𝗱𝗴𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁, 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝘆 𝗺𝗮𝗸𝗲 𝗶𝘁 𝗺𝗼𝗿𝗲 𝗶𝗺𝗽𝗼𝗿𝘁𝗮𝗻𝘁.

So no, I don’t want to build an AI-first company. I want to build one that is curious enough to explore new technology, experienced enough not to be seduced by it, and confident enough to occasionally tell a client: “You don’t need AI for this.”

Because the goal was never to use more technology. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗴𝗼𝗮𝗹 𝗶𝘀 𝘁𝗼 𝗯𝘂𝗶𝗹𝗱 𝗯𝗲𝘁𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝗯𝘂𝘀𝗶𝗻𝗲𝘀𝘀𝗲𝘀.
