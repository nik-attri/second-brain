AI Didn't Kill Jobs. It Changed What They're Worth.
Two years ago the fear was simple: AI arrives, headcount collapses, the labour market breaks. That hasn't happened. Globally the numbers are still net positive, with far more companies reporting AI led job creation than job cuts. But the impact I see on my clients is if you're hiring in AI or SaaS, who gets hired, what they get paid, and where the pipeline is quietly breaking.

What this means for how you hire
AI hasn't reduced the total amount of work. It's absorbed the routine end of it, the work that often used to justify a junior hire on its own. Now it's near instant and effectively free, so value has moved up the chain to whoever can judge the output, direct the tool, and catch it when it's wrong.

This shows up in three concrete ways.
First, your job specs are lying to you. Recruiters have started calling it seniorisation: entry level postings that quietly expect the judgement, stakeholder management and AI supervision skills that used to sit five years into someone's career. Be honest about whether you're hiring a junior at all, or hiring a senior and hoping to pay junior money for it. 

Second, cutting junior hiring feels efficient this quarter and creates a real cost three or four years out. There is no shortcut to senior judgement other than someone doing the junior work first and living with the consequences of getting it wrong. The companies short of senior talent in 2029 are making that decision right now, one skipped junior role at a time.

Third, the skills gap is now the bottleneck, not headcount. Most employers plan to invest in reskilling, yet a majority also cite the skills gap as their biggest barrier to using AI well. Hiring for AI fluency now matters as much as hiring for the underlying domain skill. 

The value of a job has shifted, not disappeared
You can see this directly in pay. The wage premium for people with strong AI skills has climbed past 60% globally and is still rising, while junior pay in the same fields has stagnated because the market has stopped rewarding the thing juniors were traditionally paid for. Companies aren't paying less for talent overall. They're paying considerably more for judgement and considerably less for output, because output stopped being scarce.

Junior roles have taken the real hit
Entry level tech postings have fallen sharply since 2023, and the pattern is consistent wherever the data has been studied: juniors in AI exposed roles losing ground while seniors in the same jobs grow. It isn't jobs disappearing the way the doomers predicted. It's the bottom rung disappearing while the rest of the ladder holds, or gets more valuable.

The practical move isn't to ignore what AI gives you. It's to keep a training pipeline alive even when the immediate return looks worse than automating the work away, because your future senior hires have to come from somewhere.
