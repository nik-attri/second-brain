A reel told me you can work out the weekday of any date in your head.
I didn't believe it. So I spent the next few weeks proving it - in 8086 assembly.

It's real, and it has a name: Conway's Doomsday Algorithm. Every year has one weekday - its "doomsday" - that a set of easy dates all land on: 4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, 12/12, and the last day of February. Find that weekday, and every other date is a short hop away.

I could have stopped at "neat trick." Instead I set a goal that scared me a little: to implement it in assembly. By hand. From scratch.

📐 CONCEPT FIRST

Before a single line of code, I worked it out on paper - century anchors, the leap-year correction, the modulo arithmetic - then dry-ran it in a spreadsheet until I could predict every branch. You cannot debug assembly you don't already understand.

⚙️ THEN C++

I wrote it in C++ first, not because it was easier, but to see the whole control flow end to end. Once the logic was proven in a language that forgives you, porting it to one that doesn't became a translation problem, not a research one.

🔧 THEN A TOOL, BECAUSE THE LOOP WAS TOO SLOW

Testing 8086 means: open DOSBox, mount c, run NASM, run the .COM, close, repeat. Dozens of times an hour. So I built a web IDE that does it in one click - a real DOSBox compiled to WebAssembly, with real NASM inside it, running entirely in the browser. No install, no server, nothing leaves your tab.

🐛 THEN THE PART NOBODY POSTS ABOUT

Weeks. Between 10 and 20 hours of debugging. Registers holding what I
didn't expect. Jumps landing where I didn't mean. A program that would print half its output and then simply... stop.

And then it printed:

    So on Year 2026 \ 08 \ 30th
    the Day of the week is Sunday

Correct. Verified by hand. I don't think I've been that happy about one line of text in my life.

🖥️ So I gave it a real home instead of leaving it as a .asm nobody opens: source on the left, a live DOS machine on the right, and a debug panel streaming the actual NASM diagnostics. It works on a phone too.
