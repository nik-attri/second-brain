As a dad of three young kids, this BBC headline caught my attention and made me wonder what the job market will look like when they eventually enter it. In my day job, I’m fortunate to be involved in enabling and shaping the adoption of AI and the technology behind it. I’m genuinely excited about what will emerge. I’m seeing real wins, but also failures and plenty of learnings along the way.

With AI cited as one possible cause, I decided to do some digging into what the wider data was telling us.

Recent UK Government and LinkedIn research found overall UK hiring was down 14% year on year, with entry level hiring falling broadly in line with the wider market. However, 30 of the 38 entry level occupations they tracked were declining. Accountants were down 29%, graphic designers 28% and software engineers 27%. Their conclusion was that, despite the headlines, hiring trends today are being driven mainly by macroeconomic conditions and business uncertainty. While AI may be contributing, the evidence doesn’t yet establish it as the cause.

There is another side to the story. PwC found UK specialist AI job postings had increased 61%, while also highlighting growing demand for human skills such as judgement, creativity and leadership. They also argue that organisations need to rethink how they develop people early in their careers, helping them build leadership, stakeholder management and complex decision making skills sooner.

As a parent, my takeaway isn’t to try to predict which jobs will survive AI. Some of the best preparation I had for working life came from developing a strong work ethic from a young age. Showing up, working hard, dealing with people, taking responsibility and learning from mistakes.

Whether our kids choose university, a trade, an apprenticeship or a career that barely exists today, those fundamentals will still matter. We also need to help them become comfortable with AI and technology while developing the human skills that complement it.

Perhaps the most important thing we can teach our kids isn’t what to become, but how to keep learning and adapting.

And for the rest of us, it’s a useful reminder to read beyond the headline, look at the underlying data and check multiple sources before deciding what it really means.
