I don't think "AI skills" will remain a separate skill for very long.

We are already seeing it move into almost every job.

A marketer isn't becoming an AI marketer.
A salesperson isn't becoming an AI salesperson.
A finance person isn't becoming an AI finance person.

They are becoming people who can do their existing job with a much larger amount of leverage.

Recent enterprise data looking at more than 1,500 organisations and 17 million AI interactions found usage spreading across different functions and levels of seniority, with particularly strong usage among early-career employees.

And another interesting finding from PwC's 2026 Jobs Barometer is that the skills required in highly AI-exposed jobs are changing roughly twice as fast as those in less-exposed jobs.

So the valuable skill isn't necessarily knowing how to use the latest AI tool, as tools will keep changing.
But understanding your work well enough to recognise where AI can create leverage.

A good salesperson doesn't need to become an AI engineer.
They need to know that they can use AI to research an account, understand previous conversations, identify buying signals, keep CRM updated without spending hours doing it manually.

The same thing applies to almost every function.

And this is why I think companies will increasingly hire people who are good at their function and unusually good at using technology to multiply their output.

Someone with 5 years of experience doing a process manually may not automatically have more leverage than someone with 2 years of experience who understands the process deeply and has redesigned half of it with AI.

The gap won't just be between people who use AI and people who don't.
It will be between people who use AI as a shortcut and people who understand how to redesign their work around it.

And that's a much more interesting workplace shift.
