Legacy manufacturers will not become AI-native by buying more software.

Engineering News-Record just profiled 1872, a new Cincinnati steel fabricator founded by engineers with backgrounds at SpaceX and GE Aviation. The company raised $15 million and is building what it calls an AI-native factory, with its own Factory OS, specialized AI agents, and robotic welding systems working together across the production process.

What caught my attention wasn't the robots, but how CEO Dan Summers described the problem with most automation efforts: companies drop automated systems into manual operating workflows and never get the full value from the technology.

Which is precisely where a lot of established manufacturers are going to get stuck with AI.

They'll buy another platform, add a few copilots, and automate isolated tasks. But material sourcing will still come from one system, estimating from another, production planning in a spreadsheet, project history in the CRM, and the actual decision logic in the heads of the few people everyone calls when something gets difficult.

1872 is starting from the other direction. It is designing sourcing, estimating, planning, logistics, machines, robots, and human expertise as one connected production system. The company says its AI agents are being trained to think like welders, buyers, material planners, and logistics professionals, and it expects the factory to reach full autonomous production in 2027.

Of course, none of that guarantees it will work as planned. Building a new steel fabrication business is a hell of a lot harder than drawing an impressive architecture diagram.

Established manufacturers still have real advantages: customers, product knowledge, engineering experience, approvals, production history, and people who understand how fabrication works under actual production constraints.

But those advantages get weaker when the company cannot retrieve and apply what it already knows.
