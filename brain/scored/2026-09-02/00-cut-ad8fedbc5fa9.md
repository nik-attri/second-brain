The real reason companies will struggle to adopt AI 👇


"Once that expertise has been adequately transferred, companies will get rid of their people and let AI agents take over."
— FAISAL HOQUE, Fast Company


That's the trade companies are actually proposing. And employees have done the math.

Hoque reports that more than a third now say they're deliberately withholding expertise because they're afraid of training their own replacement. Given what they've watched this year, that number seems low.

Which brings me to Bob in accounting.

Bob has maintained that spreadsheet for eleven years. It closes the month. It catches what your ERP misses. You've asked him three times to document it, and three times he's said he'll get to it.

You think it's stubbornness. It's arithmetic.

Look at the sequence from where he sits. Meta started capturing keystrokes and clicks this spring to teach its agents how real work gets done — then cut 8,000 people weeks later, with AI named as the reason. More than 175,000 tech jobs cut this year on roughly the same logic. 

McKinsey built Lilli specifically to move knowledge out of its experts and into something the firm owns.

Every one of those is defensible on a slide. Every one also teaches Bob exactly what happens to the person who hands over the thing only they know.

Here's the part most AI roadmaps skip.

Knowledge transfer is not an extraction problem. There is no tool that pulls eleven years of judgment out of someone who has decided not to give it to you. It requires cooperation. Cooperation requires a reason.

So the question isn't "how do we capture Bob's process."

It's "what happens to Bob after we do."

The companies getting this right answer it up front. Bob's name on the model. Bob as the reviewer when it's wrong. Bob's role redefined in writing before the first interview, not after.
