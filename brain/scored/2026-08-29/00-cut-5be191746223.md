I spent three weeks building a feature no one asked for.

It was the best mistake I've made this year.

Most SaaS founders optimize for signups, but the only metric that keeps you alive is time-to-value.

I'm currently deep in the trenches of building a new web project from scratch, and it's been a humbling reminder that having a vision is easy, but executing on the right things is incredibly hard.

I started with a massive list of features I thought were essential.

I wanted the perfect dashboard, complex AI integrations, and a UI that looked like it belonged in a museum.

Then I talked to thirty potential users.

What I found was that they didn't care about my fancy dashboard.

They were drowning in manual data entry and just wanted a way to save two hours a week.

I had to scrap three weeks of work and start over.

It hurt, but it was necessary.

Here are four things I've learned while building in the open:

First, subtraction is the ultimate competitive edge.

I used to think more features meant more value.

I was wrong.

Every new button is just more friction for the user.

I've spent the last week removing things rather than adding them.

If a feature doesn't directly solve the core pain point, it's gone.

Simplicity isn't just a design choice; it's a business strategy.

Second, design for the "Aha!" moment, not the landing page.

We spend so much time making the homepage look pretty, but the real battle is won in the first sixty seconds after a user signs up.

I'm currently experimenting with a "zero-onboarding" flow where the user gets a result before they even verify their email.

If they don't see value in the first minute, they won't come back for the second.

Third, your tech stack is a tool, not a personality trait.

I've seen founders argue for days about specific frameworks while their target market is still using spreadsheets.

I chose my stack based on what lets me ship the fastest.

Speed of iteration beats technical purity every single time.
