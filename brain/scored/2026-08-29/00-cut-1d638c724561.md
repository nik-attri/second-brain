Last week, we had an opportunity to visit villages in Malawi and met women whose homes, farms and livelihoods are swept away year after year by cyclones and flash floods.
 
Yet, they rebuild !!!!

We met smallholder farmers growing pineapples and learning to make pineapple and banana wine to strengthen their livelihoods. 

Their resilience is remarkable !!!

But what was especially striking was what we learnt in our interaction with women smallholder farmers in Chisi village. Even today... when the world is accelerating towards automation and robotics, these women manually plough their fields and sow maize and paddy. To water their crops, they make up to 20 trips every day, walking 30 minutes to the river and carrying 20-litre buckets on their heads. They nurture each plant like their own baby. They are doing everything they can to make their farms productive.

This left me wondering: If billions are committed to development, why does something as basic as access to a shared tractor or an irrigation pump remain out of reach for so many women farmers?

Development finance cannot be measured only by how much money is committed, approved or disbursed.

We should also ask:
How much of it reaches the woman farmer?
What does it enable her to do?
And does it fundamentally change the economics of her livelihood?

For smallholder women farmers, the right intervention may not be another training programme or another grant. We have seen that these women do not need charity. 
They need capital and systems that recognise their agency, reduce the constraints they face, and allow their enterprise to grow.

I am deeply grateful to H.E. Dr Joyce Banda, the team at JOYCE BANDA FOUNDATION INTERNATIONAL INC, the women and communities who welcomed us, Global Fairness Initiative and Self Employed Women's Association (SEWA) teams for making this a truly meaningful and inspiring learning experience.
