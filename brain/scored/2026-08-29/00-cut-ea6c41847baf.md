Tommy's Wealth Management Plan:

I know a guy. Let's call him Tommy.
Tommy spent 40 years in a rock band.
He made millions.
Then spent millions.
Then spent the millions the first millions were supposed to become.
A couple of ex-wives.
A couple of houses.
Several motorcycles.
Several more motorcycles he absolutely did not need.
Three sports cars.
A boat.
A bigger boat.
And one boat so large that owning it seemed less like a purchase and more like adopting a floating financial emergency.
Tommy once bought a motorcycle because it matched his sunglasses.
Another because he liked the sound it made.
And a third because, in his words:
"Life is short and this one was on sale."
His philosophy was simple:
If he wanted to go somewhere, he went.
If he wanted to buy something, he bought it.
If someone told him he shouldn't, he usually bought two.
Was this smart?
Probably not.
Was it entertaining?
Absolutely.
Tommy's estate plan is surprisingly sophisticated.
The dogs are taken care of.
Everybody else can figure it out.
"Get a job," he says.
"I got one when I was 16."
The remarkable thing is Tommy has never spent much time worrying about whether he was doing life correctly.
He wasn't trying to win a spreadsheet.
He wasn't trying to maximize some score.
He wasn't trying to have the most money when the game ended.
He was trying to have the most stories.
And on that score, he completely dominated.
Because someday nobody is going to gather around a table and say:
"Remember Tommy's account balances?"
Nobody is going to discuss his projections.
Nobody is going to debate whether he optimized everything perfectly.
Instead they'll say:
"Remember when Tommy bought a motorcycle in Arizona and drove it home?"
"Remember when he accidentally bought a boat bigger than his first house?"
"Remember when he turned every ordinary weekend into a story that probably shouldn't have happened?"
