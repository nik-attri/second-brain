On paper, my life looked perfect but the reality was far from it...

I was doing my master's at one of the top universities in Sweden. I had a solid relationship. From the outside, I looked healthy, capable, and lucky.

Behind closed doors, my body felt like it was collapsing. Sitting through a single lecture or a five-hour exam felt impossible. I was in severe physical pain, drowning in anxiety and depression, and terrified I would not even graduate.

The hardest part was the isolation. You feel completely alone in a crowded room when everyone tells you how lucky you are while you are silently wondering how to survive the day.

My family normalized chronic pain because they had lived with it their whole lives without answers, so my suffering was brushed aside. Then the person I trusted most, my partner at the time, told me my symptoms were ruining his mental health. After my official diagnosis, he suggested I was exaggerating and bought me a book to read where a doctor claims that chronic pain is in the brain/mind and you can heal yourself by reading the book and changing your mindset.  

That broke something inside me. It forced me to confront a brutal truth: no one was coming to save me. If I wanted out, I had to become the exact person I was waiting to be rescued by.

So I took the emotion out of it and put my scientist hat on.

I stopped viewing my body as an enemy and started analysing it like a complex machine. When a machine malfunctions, you do not blame it. You look for the manual. I realized nobody had ever taught me how to operate mine. I did not know how to breathe, walk, eat, or even sleep in a way that did not cause damage.

I decided to treat my recovery like a mathematical problem and a live scientific experiment. I became both the researcher and the test subject. My goal was not perfection. It was optimization: figuring out how to make this system run as efficiently as possible with the exact resources and constraints I had.
