Teachers didn’t spend years training to become safeguarding officers, mental-health workers, data analysts, administrators, social workers, attendance officers, conflict mediators and amateur lawyers who occasionally get five minutes to teach.

Then we wonder why they’re leaving.

I started teaching in 2004.

No smartphones permanently attached to hands.

No TikTok.

No AI writing an essay before you’ve finished explaining it.

No Teams messages pinging while you’re teaching.

No parent able to fire off an email about something that happened at 10:15 before you’ve even made it to lunch.

Teaching didn’t just get more complicated. The world around the classroom changed.

But instead of fundamentally rethinking the job to keep pace with that world…

we just kept bolting more crap onto the teacher.

Another responsibility.

Another system.

Another spreadsheet.

Another intervention.

Another piece of evidence.

Another thing to monitor.

Another thing to record — just in case you ever need to prove you did it.

And we’re talking about everything teachers are expected to do around teaching, without acknowledging how bloody difficult the actual teaching can be.

Because sometimes you’re handed curriculum and assessment criteria dry enough to sedate a rhinoceros, and you’re still expected to make 25 young people look intellectually electrified when somebody walks through the door. 😂

Engage them.

Challenge them.

Include them.

Differentiate.

Adapt.

Question.

Assess.

Notice who’s struggling.

Notice who’s bored.

Notice when the atmosphere changes.

Keep the room calm.

Keep the learning moving.

And make it all look effortless.

Then when you’ve finished actually teaching…

Explain it.

Evidence it.

Record it.

Upload it.

Justify it.

Just in case.

Of course teaching should have standards.

Parents should be able to ask questions.

Young people deserve an excellent education.

And when something goes wrong, it should be taken seriously.
