Right now everyone is connecting #AI #agents to everything – their inbox, their CRM, their code, their knowledge base and whatnot.

Almost nobody is asking what the agents could actually do with that access (except for InfoSec). It just works, so we move on. I mean, we need AI to be better, faster, profitable, right?!

That's a lot of blind trust to place in a machine that's a magic box for most and that's borrowing your access rights.

The agent isn't acting as some limited assistant. In the moment you grant access it can do anything you can do. Every permission you hold, it holds. And most only discover where that leads once something has gone wrong.

Enterprises will say they have controls for this. And they do – on paper. Written down on a Confluence page or a spreadsheet, assigned to someone, looked at once a year maybe.

For some controls, that static approach is fine. A policy reviewed periodically does the job.

But some controls only mean something if they are held effective at the exact moment the system is running. "This agent can't write to system xyz." "It can't send internal data after reading from an untrusted source." A checkbox in a document confirming that control exists tells you nothing about whether it was actually held on the agent's last run.

That's the gap. We're governing agents – fast, autonomous, acting in real time – with controls that are verified slowly and manually on paper.

Some controls need to move from "documented" to "enforced." Checked not in a quarterly review, but on every single action the agent takes while it takes it.

That's the shift behind trail's #runtime #enforcement for AI agents: the control isn't a line in a policy anymore – it's a rule that runs live, between the agent and its tools, and blocks the action if the rule says no.

Not trust. Enforcement.

Which of your agent controls would survive being checked at runtime and which on paper? 👀
