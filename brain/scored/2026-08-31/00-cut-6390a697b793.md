I remember years ago studying accounts, and the teacher asked us a question: “What is the greatest asset you will never find on a balance sheet?”

We all sat there in deep thought, although I suspect a few of us were probably thinking more about which pub we were going to for lunch.

Then he said: “People.”

For some reason, that has stuck with me for more than 30 years.

Not because I think we should put a value on people. In some ways, businesses already try to do that through salaries, benefits and all the other costs associated with employing someone. What stayed with me was the idea that some of the real value a person brings to a company is not always visible on a spreadsheet.

Roll the clock forward 30 years, and I have become a great believer that companies survive and thrive because of their team members' ability to adapt, use judgement and solve problems. People deal with the things the process did not predict. They notice when something is not quite right, they adjust when conditions change, and they often find a way through problems that nobody planned for.

That is why it frustrates the hell out of me when I have conversations with friends, family and colleagues who feel those very qualities are being taken away from them. They do not feel trusted, or they do not feel they have the freedom to make sensible decisions. After a while, some start to feel as though they are simply there to follow the process rather than contribute to it.

I am not really talking about micro-management versus macro-management. Both have their strengths and weaknesses depending on the situation. I am talking about something more human than that.

Some of the leaders who influenced me most gave me freedom. They let me learn, question things, try something different and sometimes get it wrong.
