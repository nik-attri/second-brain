Financial Architecture: My Unique Method for Building It

When I am asked to build financial architecture, I usually start with something that surprises people:

I don’t want to see your system.

Not because the system is unimportant.

But because I don’t want its existing logic to become my starting point.

If I begin with screens, modules, reports, workflows and existing controls, I am already being shown someone else’s interpretation of the business.

And I may unconsciously start asking:

What is wrong with this system?
Where are the gaps?
Which controls are missing?
What should we fix?

That is not where I want to begin.

I want to understand the business itself.

I want to take it apart.

Who are the parties?

What contracts exist between them?

What creates a financial obligation?

What creates an entitlement?

What data must exist before the first transaction happens?

Where does that data originate?

Which source is authoritative?

Who is allowed to create or change it?

What happens when a transaction is created?

What happens to the money?

What happens when something changes, is cancelled, refunded, disputed or manually overridden?

What should ultimately reach accounting?

And most importantly:

How do we independently prove that every financially significant event completed its full lifecycle?

Only after I understand those pieces separately do I start putting them back together.

Contract → rule → authoritative data → transaction → approval → payment → settlement → accounting → reconciliation → outstanding balance → closure.

At that point, I have my own independent model of how the financial architecture should work.

Only then do I want to see the existing architecture.

I overlay my model onto what already exists in the organisation.
