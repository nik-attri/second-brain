What Happens to Our Thinking When AI Starts Thinking for Us?

While I am waiting for my cue to walk onto the stage, I thought about the final sentences of my keynote once again. For a moment, I consider changing them.
Are they too uncomfortable? Will people get afraid or wake up. To recognise what is already changing inside themselves. And then to change something.
I know how difficult behavioural change is.

As a psychologist, I have helped people stop smoking, change deeply rooted eating rituals and break patterns that no longer served them. I have also had to confront my own conditioning.

One thing becomes clear again and again: people rarely change because they receive more information. They change when they recognise something about themselves.

That is why I believe the most important AI debate is no longer whether AI will change our jobs. It already is.

The deeper question is: What happens to our thinking when AI starts thinking for us?

When an AI system produces an answer that is fluent, coherent and confident, our brains can mistake fluency for credibility. The easier an answer feels to process, the less cognitive effort we may invest in challenging it. Over time, convenience can become reliance.

We know versions of this phenomenon as automation bias and cognitive offloading. The new dimension is that AI does not merely calculate for us. It speaks to us.

The more convincing the machine sounds, the more conscious the human needs to become.
The biggest AI risk is not that machines will think too much. It is that humans will think too little.

The first AI wave automated tasks. The second augmented human capabilities. We are now moving towards AI agents that orchestrate workflows, prepare decisions and increasingly act. This changes leadership.

AI can generate options. We must decide which consequences we are willing to accept.
AI can recognise patterns. We must decide which patterns matter.
